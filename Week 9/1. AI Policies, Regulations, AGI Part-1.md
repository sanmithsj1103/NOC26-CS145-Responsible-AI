# AI Policies, Regulations, AGI - Part 1

### Introduction

The rapid evolution of Artificial Intelligence, particularly generative AI, presents both unprecedented opportunities and complex challenges for society, governments, and industry. As AI systems become more sophisticated and integrated into daily life, critical questions arise regarding their ethical deployment, regulatory frameworks, and societal impact. This lecture delves into these multifaceted issues, exploring the current landscape of AI policy, the varying approaches taken by different nations, and the ongoing debate surrounding concepts like Artificial General Intelligence (AGI). From the practical application of AI in government decision-making to the philosophical implications of its future trajectory, understanding these dynamics is crucial for students and professionals alike who seek to shape a responsible and beneficial AI-powered future. The discussion highlights the need for interdisciplinary approaches, combining technical expertise with policy insights, to navigate this transformative era effectively.

The Block Center at Carnegie Mellon University, with its diverse faculty, stands at the forefront of addressing these challenges. Their work spans from practical partnerships with state governments on generative AI use cases to fundamental research on AI agent reliability and the implications of open-source AI. This comprehensive perspective underscores the importance of considering AI not just as a technological marvel, but as a powerful tool that requires careful governance, thoughtful integration, and continuous evaluation to ensure it serves humanity's best interests. The insights shared offer a roadmap for understanding the intricate interplay between technological advancement and societal responsibility in the age of AI.

### Core Concepts

| Term | Definition | Significance |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`AGI (Artificial General Intelligence)`** | A hypothetical AI with human-like cognitive abilities, capable of understanding, learning, and applying intelligence across a wide range of tasks. | Its definition is debated; the lecture emphasizes focusing on current AI capabilities and societal benefits rather than speculative future states. |
| **`Brussels Effect`** | The phenomenon where the European Union's regulations, due to the size and importance of its market, become de facto global standards. | Expected to influence global AI policy, as companies serving the EU market will likely comply with the EU AI Act universally. |
| **`Vertical Regulation`** | Sector-specific laws and regulations that govern the use of AI within particular industries (e.g., healthcare, finance, employment). | Utilizes existing legal frameworks, often with minor adjustments, to address AI applications in established domains. |
| **`Horizontal Regulation`** | Broad, cross-sector laws that apply to AI systems regardless of their specific application domain, often categorizing AI by risk level. | Aims to create overarching principles for AI governance, exemplified by the EU AI Act's risk-based approach. |
| **`India Stack`** | A set of open APIs and digital public goods that aim to unlock the economic primitives of identity, data, and payments at scale. | Represents a government-led approach to digital infrastructure, where the government owns individual data to enable private sector service competition. |
| **`AI Literacy`** | The understanding of AI concepts, capabilities, limitations, and ethical implications necessary for individuals to navigate an AI-driven world. | Crucial for bridging the digital divide, reducing fear, and enabling informed participation in an AI-powered society. |
| **`Sovereign AI`** | The concept of a nation developing and controlling its own AI capabilities, including models trained on its own data and languages. | Important for ensuring adequate representation of diverse cultures and languages, preventing reliance on foreign AI systems. |
| **`Fairness Impossibility Theorem`** | The mathematical proof that it is impossible to satisfy all desirable fairness metrics simultaneously in an AI system. | Highlights the inherent trade-offs in designing fair AI, requiring careful consideration of which fairness metrics are most appropriate for a given context. |
| **`AI Value Chains`** | The interconnected stages and entities involved in the development, deployment, and use of AI systems, often spanning multiple jurisdictions. | Illustrates the complexity of assigning obligations and ensuring compliance when AI components and services cross international borders. |

### Detailed Analysis

#### 1. Research Frontiers at the Block Center

The Block Center at Carnegie Mellon University is engaged in a variety of impactful AI research projects, reflecting the diverse challenges and opportunities presented by the technology.
- **Generative AI in Government Decision-Making**: A practical partnership with the state of Pennsylvania focuses on identifying and prioritizing internal-facing use cases for generative AI within government. This involves problem formulation, data governance, and policy considerations, highlighting the interdisciplinary nature of AI deployment.
- **Reliability of AI Agents**: This technical project, often in collaboration with organizations like NIST (National Institutes of Standards and Technology), addresses the critical question of how to assess the reliability and measure the performance of AI agents, especially those based on deep learning transformer architectures.
- **Open Source AI Evaluation**: Research explores what it means for AI to be "open source" and how to evaluate such systems. This includes developing transparency indices, similar to those for traditional open-source software, to understand the implications for evaluation and responsible AI.
- **Future of Work**: The Workforce Supply Chain Project investigates how AI deployment will augment or substitute human skills, analyzing the implications for individuals, firms, and policymakers.

#### 2. Perspectives on Artificial General Intelligence (AGI)

The concept of AGI remains a subject of significant debate and varying interpretations.
- **Definitional Ambiguity**: The speaker expresses skepticism about a clear definition of AGI, likening it to "beauty in the eye of the beholder." The focus is shifted from predicting AGI's arrival to understanding the continuous evolution of AI capabilities.
- **Focus on Current Capabilities**: Rather than speculating on AGI, the emphasis is on maximizing the utility of current AI advancements. This involves deploying AI in ways that enhance productivity and address societally consequential problems, such as climate change, drug discovery, and education.
- **Societal Benefit**: The primary concern is how to foster the appropriate growth and deployment of AI to ensure it is societally beneficial, rather than fixating on a distant, ill-defined future state.

#### 3. Governmental Approaches to AI Regulation

Governments worldwide are grappling with how to regulate AI, adopting diverse models and engaging in intergovernmental discussions.
- **Intergovernmental Cooperation**: AI value chains often span multiple jurisdictions (e.g., a US AI company, a German software company, deployed in various cities). This necessitates international agreements to facilitate trade and address obligations when errors occur.
- **Trade in Compute Services**: Compute power is emerging as a tradable resource, with countries rich in energy potentially hosting data centers and trading services.
- **Harmonization of Policies**: Efforts are underway to harmonize policies and frameworks across different layers of the AI stack (compute, data, model, applications).
- **NIST AI Risk Management Framework**: Frameworks like NIST's are gaining international adoption, with agreements like the Singapore-US crosswalk between their respective AI verification architectures.
- **Three Models of Regulation**:
- **Market-Based (US)**: Tends to favor innovation, with existing laws governing specific sectors.
- **Human Rights-Based (EU)**: Prioritizes fundamental rights, leading to comprehensive horizontal regulations like the EU AI Act, which is expected to have a "Brussels Effect" globally.
- **State-Focused (China)**: Emphasizes state control and national interests.
- **India's Approach**: India appears to be leaning towards a market-based approach, though companies serving the European market will still need to comply with the EU AI Act. The "India Stack" exemplifies a unique model where the government provides foundational digital infrastructure (like Aadhaar for identity and UPI for payments), owning individual data to enable private sector competition in service delivery without data ownership. This contrasts with the US model where private companies often own vast amounts of individual data.

#### 4. Career Transitions and Future Skills

The speaker's personal journey from engineering to policy, management science, and AI highlights the value of intellectual curiosity and interdisciplinary skills.
- **Interdisciplinary Foundation**: A background in optimization, computing, and statistics proved highly beneficial for the rise of machine learning and AI.
- **Intellectual Curiosity**: The key takeaway for students is to remain intellectually curious and continuously seek new knowledge.
- **"CS Plus One" Strategy**: Students are advised to develop deep expertise in one area (e.g., Computer Science) and then cross-train in another complementary field (e.g., policy, economics, ethics) based on their interests. This prepares them for evolving roles in the AI landscape.

#### 5. Future Directions in AI Research and Application

Predicting the exact future of AI is challenging, but certain areas show significant promise.
- **On-Device AI**: Expect major advancements in AI capabilities that run fully disconnected from the cloud, directly on devices like phones or iPads. This will unlock new application opportunities, especially for startups and research.
- **AI and Energy**: Addressing the energy consumption of AI is a critical challenge. AI can be both a contributor to the problem and a solution for decarbonization efforts. This area presents significant opportunities for innovation.
- **Caution Against Speculation**: The rapid pace of AI development (e.g., from GPT-2 to current generative AI) makes precise predictions difficult, underscoring the need for adaptability.

#### 6. AI Literacy and Bridging the Digital Divide

Ensuring broad AI literacy is crucial to prevent an "AI divide" and address public fears.
- **Global Challenge**: AI literacy is a significant problem even in developed nations like the US.
- **Access and Cost**: Key components for AI literacy campaigns include ensuring widespread internet access (e.g., India's cheap data rates), reducing the cost of making AI available, and deploying it at scale.
- **Localized and Relevant Content**: AI education should be provided in local languages (e.g., Tamil, Malayalam, Hindi) and tied to applications that matter to people's daily lives, such as agriculture, education, or healthcare.
- **Educational Tools**: Examples like Khan Amigo (AI combined with Khan Academy) demonstrate how AI tutors can significantly enhance education, especially in regions with large student populations and limited teacher resources.

#### 7. AI as an Arms Race

The dual-use nature of AI raises concerns about its potential as a weapon.
- **Military Applications**: AI is already being used in military contexts, combined with technologies like drones, for offensive capabilities, information warfare, and cyber warfare (e.g., Israel-Gaza, Ukraine-Russia conflicts).
- **Calls for Treaties**: Prominent AI researchers, such as Yoshua Bengio, advocate for international treaties between major powers (e.g., US and China) to prevent an AI arms race, similar to nuclear non-proliferation efforts.
- **National Models**: Different national models for AI development, as discussed by Kai-Fu Lee, highlight the geopolitical implications of AI.

#### 8. Data Representation and Bias

The data used to train AI models significantly impacts their fairness and representativeness.
- **Bias from Training Data**: Models trained on data from platforms like Reddit, which may be predominantly English or reflect specific demographics, can perpetuate biases and fail to represent segments of the population that do not share their data or are not active on those platforms.
- **Non-Digitized Languages/Populations**: If languages or populations are not adequately digitized, AI models trained on existing data will inherently lack their representation.
- **Importance of Sovereign AI**: To address this, it is crucial for nations like India, with its linguistic diversity, to develop its own "sovereign AI" trained on local data sources, rather than solely relying on models from global tech giants.

#### 9. Career Advice for Data Science Graduates

For students finishing data science degrees, the focus should be on operationalizing AI.
- **Operationalizing AI**: The real opportunity lies not just in developing new models, but in figuring out how to effectively deploy AI within organizations to solve real-world problems and create value. This includes improving existing services (e.g., railways, healthcare, education) or enhancing business processes.
- **Entrepreneurial Opportunities**: There are vast entrepreneurial opportunities in applying AI to specific industry challenges.
- **Passion-Driven Choices**: Ultimately, career choices should be driven by what genuinely excites the individual.

#### 10. Fairness Scores and Public Welfare

The importance of fairness scores in deploying generative AI models for public welfare is context-dependent and complex.
- **Application Specificity**: The relevance of fairness scores varies greatly depending on the application (e.g., loan granting, employment, healthcare).
- **Existing Laws**: Many existing anti-discriminatory laws (e.g., fair credit reporting) already provide a framework for evaluating AI fairness.
- **Thresholds and Trade-offs**: Organizations deploying AI must set acceptable thresholds for fairness, robustness, and reliability. The "fairness impossibility theorem" means that not all fairness metrics can be optimized simultaneously, requiring careful selection based on the application's goals.
- **Decision Support vs. Automation**: The role of AI (pure decision support vs. full automation) and the degree of human involvement ("human in the loop") are critical factors in determining acceptable error rates and accountability.

#### 11. AI Regulation and Enforcement Challenges

The perceived delay in AI regulation enforcement stems from a need to balance innovation with governance and the distinction between different types of regulation.
- **Vertical vs. Horizontal Regulation**:
- **Vertical Regulation**: Existing laws in specific sectors (e.g., employment, finance) can often be adapted to govern AI use. There is "no AI exception" to complying with these laws.
- **Horizontal Regulation**: Broader, cross-sector regulations (like the EU AI Act) categorize AI by risk. Jurisdictions delaying such horizontal regulation often do so to foster innovation, relying on vertical laws in the interim.
- **Accountability for Errors**: When AI causes harm, the question of who is penalized is crucial. In sectors with existing laws, the organization deploying the AI is typically liable. The challenge arises in unregulated "wild west" scenarios where liability is unclear.
- **Responsible Deployment**: The focus should be on deploying AI responsibly, with clear understanding of error costs, liability, and appropriate measurement systems.

#### 12. Trust in Data: Government vs. Industry

The question of trusting governments or industry with personal data reveals different societal models.
- **Industry Model (e.g., US, Social Media)**: Users often exchange personal data for "free" services (e.g., WhatsApp, Google Search). This advertising-based model leads to companies accumulating vast data repositories and forming oligopolies.
- **Government Model (e.g., India Stack)**: In India, the government (via Aadhaar) owns individual biometric data, creating a public infrastructure. This allows private companies to compete in providing services (e.g., UPI apps) without owning the underlying individual data.
- **Trade-offs**: Both models involve a form of trust. The choice reflects societal preferences regarding who controls sensitive personal information and how digital ecosystems are structured. Blockchain offers a theoretical alternative for distributed trust, but its economic viability at scale is debated.

#### 13. Quality of Service: Current vs. Aspirational

The desired "quality of service" for AI outputs depends heavily on context and whether the system is viewed as static or dynamic.
- **Context-Dependent**: For factual queries based on current data, the AI should provide an accurate reflection of the present state.
- **Aspirational for Dynamic Systems**: When AI is used in dynamic systems, especially for purposes like education or motivating future generations, an aspirational approach is often preferred. The output should aim to inspire and shape future states, recognizing that the AI's response can influence subsequent actions and perceptions.
