### Core Concepts

| Term | Definition | Significance |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`Identity Labels`** | Predefined lists of words representing various social identities, such as gender, race, or sexual orientation. | Used as reference points to measure associations and potential biases in the dataset. |
| **`Deduplication`** | The process of identifying and removing redundant or identical entries from a dataset. | Ensures data quality and prevents overcounting, leading to more accurate statistical analyses. |
| **`Stop Words`** | Common words (e.g., "a," "the," "is") that carry little semantic meaning and are often removed during text preprocessing. | Their removal helps focus analysis on more meaningful content words, improving the signal-to-noise ratio. |
| **`Tokenization`** | The process of breaking down a text into smaller units, such as words or subwords, called tokens. | Essential first step in most NLP tasks, enabling individual word analysis and frequency counting. |

| **`Bigram Probability`** | The likelihood of a sequence of two words (a bigram) appearing together in a corpus. | Captures co-occurrence patterns between words, crucial for understanding contextual relationships and PMI. |
| **`Pointwise Mutual Information (PMI)`** | A measure of association between two words, indicating how much more frequently they co-occur than expected by chance. | A key metric for quantifying the strength of association between identity terms and other words, revealing potential biases. |
| **`Corpus`** | A large and structured set of texts, typically stored electronically, used for linguistic analysis. | The primary data source from which word frequencies and co-occurrence patterns are extracted for bias analysis. |

#### 1. Data Loading and Initial Preparation

The first step in this bias detection exercise involves loading the necessary data. The primary dataset used is the SNLI 1.0 train dataset, which is loaded into a Pandas DataFrame. It was observed that the dataset contained a number of duplicate entries. Removing these duplicates is essential to ensure that statistical calculations are accurate and not skewed by redundant data points, leading to a cleaner and more reliable corpus for analysis.

#### 2. Text Preprocessing for Analysis

To prepare the textual data for meaningful statistical analysis, several cleaning and normalization steps are performed. These steps aim to reduce noise and standardize the text, which is vital for accurate probability estimations, especially with a limited dataset size.

* **Stop Word Removal**: Common words that typically do not carry significant meaning (e.g., "a," "an," "the," "is") are identified and removed. This process utilizes a predefined list of English stop words, often sourced from libraries like NLTK.
```python
# Conceptual function for stop word removal
import nltk
from nltk.corpus import stopwords
import string

def remove_stopwords_and_punctuation(sentence):
stop_words = set(stopwords.words('english'))
punctuations = set(string.punctuation)
tokens = sentence.lower().split() # Lowercase and split into tokens
filtered_tokens = [
word for word in tokens
if word not in stop_words and all(char not in punctuations for char in word)
]
return " ".join(filtered_tokens)
```
* **Punctuation Removal**: Punctuation marks (e.g., commas, periods, question marks) are also removed from the text. This ensures that words are not treated as distinct entities simply because of an attached punctuation mark.
* **Lowercasing**: All characters in the text are converted to lowercase. This standardizes words, treating "Person" and "person" as the same token, which is critical for accurate frequency counts.
* **Tokenization**: Sentences are broken down into individual words or "tokens." The NLTK library's word tokenizer is employed for this purpose. This step transforms a continuous string of text into a list of discrete words, making them amenable to further processing. For example, "A person is outdoors on a horse." becomes `['a', 'person', 'is', 'outdoors', 'on', 'a', 'horse', '.']`. After stop word and punctuation removal, this might become `['person', 'outdoors', 'horse']`.

These preprocessing steps collectively transform raw text into a clean, standardized format, ready for the computation of word probabilities.

#### 3. Computing Unigram and Bigram Probabilities

The core of PMI calculation relies on the probabilities of individual words (unigrams) and pairs of words (bigrams).

* **Unigram Probabilities**: To compute unigram probabilities, all the processed text from the corpus is combined. The frequency of every unique word is then counted using a `Counter` object. The top 20 most common unigrams are often inspected to get an initial sense of the dataset's vocabulary, revealing words like "man," "women," "dog," "black," "shirt," "boy," "girl" after stop words have been removed. Words with a frequency less than a certain threshold (e.g., 15) are discarded to focus on more statistically significant terms.
```python
# Conceptual code for computing unigram frequencies
from collections import Counter

def compute_unigram_frequencies(corpus_tokens):
all_words = [word for sentence_tokens in corpus_tokens for word in sentence_tokens]
unigram_counts = Counter(all_words)
# Filter out low-frequency words
filtered_unigram_counts = {word: count for word, count in unigram_counts.items() if count >= 15}
return filtered_unigram_counts
```
* **Bigram Probabilities**: For bigram probabilities, the lecture discusses two approaches: treating sentence 1 and sentence 2 of the SNLI dataset as a single combined sentence, or treating them independently. For this exercise, the sentences are treated independently. This means that if the dataset has N sentences, the bigram probabilities are computed across 2N effective sentences. Similar to unigrams, bigram pairs with a frequency less than a certain threshold (e.g., 10) are discarded to ensure statistical relevance.
```python
# Conceptual code for computing bigram frequencies
def compute_bigram_frequencies(corpus_tokens):
bigram_counts = Counter()
for sentence_tokens in corpus_tokens:
for i in range(len(sentence_tokens) - 1):
bigram_counts[(sentence_tokens[i], sentence_tokens[i+1])] += 1
# Filter out low-frequency bigrams
filtered_bigram_counts = {bigram: count for bigram, count in bigram_counts.items() if count >= 10}
return filtered_bigram_counts
```

#### 4. Calculating Pointwise Mutual Information (PMI)

With unigram and bigram probabilities in hand, the next step is to compute the PMI score. PMI quantifies the statistical association between two words, indicating how much more likely they are to co-occur than if they were independent. A higher PMI score suggests a stronger, non-random association.

The PMI function takes two words (`word1`, `word2`), the unigram frequency dictionary, and the bigram frequency dictionary as input.
The formula for PMI(word1, word2) is:
`log2 ( P(word1, word2) / (P(word1) * P(word2)) )`

Where:
* `P(word1, word2)` is the bigram probability of `word1` and `word2` occurring together. This is calculated as `frequency(word1, word2) / total_number_of_bigrams`.
* `P(word1)` is the unigram probability of `word1`. This is calculated as `frequency(word1) / total_number_of_unigrams`.
* `P(word2)` is the unigram probability of `word2`. This is calculated as `frequency(word2) / total_number_of_unigrams`.

```python
# Conceptual function to compute PMI score
import math

def calculate_pmi(word1, word2, unigram_freq, bigram_freq, total_unigrams, total_bigrams):
freq_w1 = unigram_freq.get(word1, 0)
freq_w2 = unigram_freq.get(word2, 0)
freq_w1_w2 = bigram_freq.get((word1, word2), 0)

if freq_w1 == 0 or freq_w2 == 0 or freq_w1_w2 == 0:
return 0 # Or a very low negative number to indicate no co-occurrence

p_w1 = freq_w1 / total_unigrams
p_w2 = freq_w2 / total_unigrams
p_w1_w2 = freq_w1_w2 / total_bigrams

if p_w1 == 0 or p_w2 == 0: # Avoid division by zero
return 0

pmi = math.log2(p_w1_w2 / (p_w1 * p_w2))
return pmi
```

#### 5. Detecting Bias through PMI Analysis

Once the PMI scores can be computed, the analysis shifts to identifying biases. This involves calculating the PMI score between each identity label (e.g., "women," "man," "boys," "girls") and every other unigram in the corpus. The words are then sorted based on their PMI scores with respect to a given identity label.

The results vividly illustrate the presence of stereotypes:
* For **"women"**, highly associated words include "buys," "ordered," "gossiping," "bikinis."
* For **"men"**, words like "refueling," "own," "steal," "arguing," "turban" show strong associations.
* For **"boys"**, "playing" is a top association.
* For **"girls"**, "cheerleading" stands out.

Further analysis involves looking at words uniquely associated with one gender group but not another. For instance, words like "bikinis," "blonde hair," "braids," "colorful," "cheering," and "brushes" are found to be unique to the female set, while "angry," "army," "backpack," and "wrestle" are unique to the male set. These distinct associations are clear indicators of stereotypical representations within the dataset.

The exercise also extends to examining LGBT identity terms. For terms like "LGBT," words such as "couple," "men," "pride," and "rights" show significant associations. This demonstrates how biases, even those related to underrepresented groups, can emerge from the data. The findings underscore a critical point: human-generated data, especially from crowdsourced annotations, inherently carries and perpetuates societal biases. This highlights the necessity for careful instruction design for crowdsourcing workers and proactive measures to prevent such implicit biases from inadvertently influencing datasets, which are ultimately used to train and evaluate AI models.
