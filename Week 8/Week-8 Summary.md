### Introduction

As artificial intelligence systems increasingly permeate critical sectors like healthcare, finance, and autonomous systems, the imperative to understand their decision-making processes has never been greater. This week's lectures delve into the crucial concepts of interpretability, transparency, and probing in AI, which are essential for transforming opaque "black box" models into understandable and trustworthy tools. The core challenge, often termed the "alignment problem," is to ensure that an AI system's actions perfectly align with human intentions and values. When AI systems behave unexpectedly, exhibit biases, or generate inappropriate content, it signals a misalignment that can erode trust and lead to harmful outcomes.

The pursuit of interpretability is fundamentally about shedding light on the internal workings of these complex models. It's not enough to know *what* a model predicts; we must also understand *why* it makes a particular prediction. This understanding is vital for debugging errors, ensuring fairness, identifying and mitigating biases, and preventing deceptive behaviors. By exploring various techniques—from visual attribution methods for images to feature contribution analysis for tabular data, and even novel approaches like codebook features—we aim to equip ourselves with the tools to monitor AI behavior, ensure accountability, and ultimately build more reliable and ethical AI systems that can be deployed responsibly across all domains.

### Core Concepts

| Term | Definition | Significance |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`Interpretability`** | The degree to which a human can understand the cause of a decision made by an AI model, revealing *how* and *why* decisions are made. | Essential for building trust, debugging models, and ensuring ethical and fair AI behavior, especially in high-stakes applications. |
| **`Transparency`** | The property of an AI system that allows its internal mechanisms and decision-making processes to be easily understood and inspected. | Enables stakeholders to scrutinize model logic, identify biases, and verify compliance with regulations, fostering accountability. |
| **`Alignment Problem`** | The challenge of ensuring that an AI system's actions and goals align with human intentions and values, preventing undesirable or harmful behaviors. | Interpretability and transparency are key tools to address this, ensuring AI systems perform as intended and do not exhibit harmful behaviors. |
| **`Feature Attribution`** | A broader concept explaining individual predictions by attributing importance to each input feature based on its impact on the prediction. | Applicable across various data types (text, tabular, image) to understand which parts of the input are most influential in a decision. |
| **`LIME`** | Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations; a technique that explains individual predictions of any black-box model by approximating it locally with an interpretable model. | Offers local fidelity, explaining specific predictions without needing to understand the complex global model, making it versatile. |
| **`SHAP`** | SHapley Additive exPlanations; a game-theoretic approach that assigns an importance value to each feature for a particular prediction, based on Shapley values. | Provides a unified measure of feature importance, accounting for interactions between features, offering robust explanations. |
| **`GradCAM`** | Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping; a technique for producing visual explanations for CNNs by using gradients flowing into the final convolutional layer. | Highlights important regions in an image that contribute to a specific class prediction, making CNNs more transparent. |
| **`Trojan Attack`** | A type of adversarial attack where hidden functionalities are embedded into a model, which, when triggered by specific inputs, cause a sudden and dangerous change in behavior. | Poses a significant security risk, as it can lead to unpredictable and malicious actions by AI systems, undermining their reliability. |
| **`Codebook Features`** | A method that incorporates discrete, learnable entries into neural network layers, converting continuous activations into discrete codes. | Enhances interpretability and controllability by providing a structured, discrete representation of internal states, enabling steering. |
| **`Probing`** | A class of methodologies used to check whether specific information or capabilities are encoded within a model's internal representations. | Helps determine if a model genuinely performs necessary subtasks or merely achieves high accuracy through shortcuts, revealing its true understanding. |

### Detailed Analysis

#### 1. The Imperative of Interpretability and Transparency
The fundamental drive behind studying interpretability and transparency, as discussed in **Interpretability - I** and **Interpretability - II**, is to tackle the "alignment problem." This critical challenge involves ensuring that an AI system's actions and goals are perfectly aligned with human intentions and values. When AI models operate as opaque "black boxes," their decisions can be inscrutable, leading to potential issues such as bias, unfairness, or unexpected behaviors. Transparency aims to provide clarity into a model's inner workings, allowing us to understand *why* it makes certain predictions. This understanding is crucial for:
- **Clarity**: Demystifying complex models and making their decision-making processes understandable to humans.
- **Understanding Change**: Comprehending why a model's internal representations or outputs change when its architecture or data inputs are modified.
- **Monitoring and Hazard Prevention**: Detecting and mitigating deceptive behaviors or other hazards that might arise from an uninterpretable system.
By making models transparent, we can monitor their performance, debug issues, and build greater trust in their capabilities, which is vital for responsible AI deployment.

#### 2. Model-Agnostic and Specific Interpretability Techniques
This week explored a range of techniques to interpret AI models, from general, model-agnostic approaches to those tailored for specific architectures like Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs).
- **LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations)**, introduced in **Interpretability - I**, is a powerful technique designed to explain the predictions of *any* black-box machine learning model. It is "model-agnostic" and works across various data types. LIME's core principle is "local fidelity," meaning it provides accurate explanations for a specific prediction by approximating the complex model locally with a simpler, interpretable model (e.g., linear regression).
- **SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations)**, also from **Interpretability - I**, is a game-theoretic approach that assigns an "SHAP value" to each feature for a particular prediction. Based on Shapley values, SHAP provides a unified measure of feature importance, crucially accounting for interactions between features, which simpler methods often overlook.
- **Pixel Attribution Methods**, detailed in **Interpretability - I**, are primarily used for image-based AI models. These methods highlight specific pixels or regions in an image that were most influential in a neural network's classification decision, often creating a "heatmap."
- **Saliency Maps** are a common form of pixel attribution. The general workflow involves a forward pass, computing the gradient of the class score with respect to input pixels, and then visualizing these gradients.
- **SmoothGrad** enhances saliency maps by averaging maps from multiple noisy versions of the input, producing clearer and more robust visualizations.
- **Guided Backpropagation** modifies the backpropagation process to only allow positive gradients to propagate, highlighting pixels that positively contribute to the target class activation.
- **GradCAM (Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping)**, covered in **Interpretability - I**, is a popular method for CNNs. It uses gradients flowing into the final convolutional layer to compute a "coarse localization map" that highlights important image regions for a specific classification.
- **Feature Visualization**, from **Interpretability - I**, is a technique to understand what a model's internal components (like individual neurons or channels) are detecting. It synthesizes an input image that maximally activates a specific component, revealing the visual patterns or features that component is sensitive to.
- **ProtoPNet (Prototypical Part Network)**, discussed in **Interpretability - II**, aims to classify images by comparing parts of a test image to learned "prototypical parts." This provides a visual explanation by showing *which* parts of the input image were most influential, offering transparency into the model's reasoning.
- **Mechanistic Interpretability** and **Top-Down Interpretability**, introduced in **Interpretability - II**, represent two distinct approaches. Mechanistic interpretability is a bottom-up approach that reverse-engineers individual neurons and their circuits to explain their function (e.g., how specific parts of a car excite or inhibit "car detector" neurons). Top-down interpretability, conversely, seeks to locate information within a model at a higher level, often by analyzing global activity or representational spaces, providing a more abstract view suitable for very large models.
- **Interpretability for Text Data**, from **Interpretability - I**, extends these concepts to text. "Token saliency" highlights which words or tokens contribute most to a classifier's prediction, often using gradient magnitude with respect to token embeddings.

#### 3. Probing and Detecting Misalignment
Beyond understanding *how* models work, it's crucial to detect when they might be dishonest or deceptive. **Interpretability - II** highlights that AI models can exhibit various forms of misalignment:
- **Honesty**: Providing factually incorrect information.
- **Deception**: Expressing intentions that hide true capabilities or malicious goals.
- **Fact Editing**: Presenting false information as truth.
**Probing**, a specific methodology from **Interpretability - II**, assesses what information is encoded in a model's internal representations. It asks whether the model's internal state contains enough information to perform a specific task, or if it's merely achieving high accuracy through shortcuts, potentially "deleting information" that humans deem crucial. Probing helps verify if the model retains the distinctions and information that humans care about, ensuring it solves problems meaningfully.

#### 4. Adversarial Threats: Trojan Attacks and Data Poisoning
A significant security concern discussed in **Interpretability - II** is the **Trojan Attack**. This type of adversarial attack embeds hidden functionalities into a model, which, when triggered by specific inputs, cause a sudden and dangerous change in behavior.
- **Mechanism**: Adversaries implement hidden functionalities that, when triggered, cause a sudden or dangerous change in the model's behavior (e.g., a driverless car ignoring a stop sign with a specific pattern).
- **How Trojans Get In**: Malicious data can be embedded in public datasets, or pre-trained models from sharing libraries might contain Trojans. **Data Poisoning** is a common method, where a subtle trigger (e.g., a small pattern) is injected into a small fraction of the training data. When the model encounters an input with this trigger, it misclassifies it according to the adversary's intent. These triggers can be visually subtle and hard to detect manually.
- **Defending Against Trojan Attacks**: Detecting Trojans is challenging due to the complexity of neural networks. **Neural Cleanse** is a defense mechanism that aims to detect and reverse-engineer Trojan triggers by searching for minimal input perturbations that cause misclassification. It identifies an "anomaly index" – a substantially smaller trigger compared to others – to indicate a Trojan. Once a trigger is identified, the Trojan's functionality can be removed by pruning or modifying the affected neurons.

#### 5. Codebook Features for Enhanced Interpretability and Control
**Interpretability Hands-on - Part 1** introduces **Codebook Features** as a novel approach to enhance interpretability and controllability.
- **Mechanism**: Codebook features integrate discrete, learnable entries into neural network layers, converting continuous activations into discrete codes through **vector quantization**. This creates a structured bottleneck, making internal states more understandable. The learning process involves a modified loss function that includes an L2 norm between the input to the codebook and its output, alongside the neural network's existing loss. A crucial `Stop Gradient (SG)` notation ensures that gradients do not flow back through the codebook input, allowing the codebook entries to be learned effectively without disrupting main network training.
- **Steering Model Generation**: One of the most compelling applications is the ability to "steer" the model's generation towards specific topics. By identifying and activating particular **topic codes** (e.g., code 18 for "video game"), the model can generate text aligned with the specified theme, even from a neutral prompt.
- **Setting Up and Verifying Hooked Models**: To implement this, the original model is wrapped into a "hooked model" using functions like `wrap_codebook` and `convert_to_hooked_model`. A critical verification step involves comparing the outputs of the original and hooked models to ensure the hooking process hasn't introduced unintended changes.
- **Quantitative Evaluation**: While qualitative observation shows steering works, quantitative evaluation is essential. Metrics like `topic_in_prompt_completion` (how often the target topic appears in steered generations) and `topic_in_prompt_completion_average` (how often it appears across all generations, including unsteered) are used. Comparing these to a baseline (unsteered model) provides robust evidence of the effectiveness and specificity of steering.

#### 6. Interpreting Codebook Models in Sequence Prediction (TOC-FSM)
**Interpretability Hands-on - Part 2** applies codebook models to the **TOC-FSM (Finite State Machine)** dataset, a synthetic dataset designed for controlled evaluation of sequence prediction.
- **Setup**: A GPT-NEO-X model is augmented with codebook features and applied to sequences generated by an FSM. States are tokenized (e.g., state 18 becomes "1, 8").
- **Code Purity and Superposition**: **Code purity** measures how exclusively a learned code is associated with a single input pattern. The analysis revealed that the first MLP layer exhibits very high purity for bigram and trigram inputs, suggesting that codebook training effectively resolves the **superposition problem** (where multiple distinct inputs map to the same hidden representation) in early layers. Purity tends to decline in higher layers as codes represent more abstract features.
- **Intervening on State Codes**: The tutorial demonstrated how to intervene on state codes and measure their influence using **JS Divergence (JSD)**. By generating valid bigram inputs with prefixes and applying various "patching" configurations (e.g., at Layer 0's attention or Layer 1's MLP), the impact of modifying specific internal codes on the model's output probability distributions can be quantified.
- **Grouping Inputs by Codes**: Further analysis investigated whether states grouped together by codes in latent layers exhibit more similar output distributions. The findings showed that trigram input pairs grouped by the same code consistently exhibited significantly smaller JSD values compared to randomly selected pairs, confirming that the model effectively clusters semantically related inputs.
