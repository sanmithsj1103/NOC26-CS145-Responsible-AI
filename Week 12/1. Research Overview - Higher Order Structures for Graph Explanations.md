# Research Overview: Higher Order Structures for Graph Explanations

### Introduction

Graphs are ubiquitous in modern data science, representing complex relationships in diverse fields from molecular structures for drug discovery to intricate social networks and financial transactions. Their natural ability to model interconnected data makes them invaluable, especially when processed by Graph Neural Networks (GNNs). However, the increasing deployment of GNNs in high-stakes applications, such as fraud detection and medical diagnostics, necessitates a critical understanding of their decision-making processes. Without interpretability, GNNs risk learning spurious correlations, leading to unreliable or even harmful predictions. This research delves into the fundamental limitations of traditional graph representations and introduces a novel framework, FORGE, which leverages higher-order structures called cell complexes to enhance the explainability and trustworthiness of GNN predictions.

The core challenge lies in the inherent limitation of graphs to model only pairwise relationships between nodes. This restricts GNNs from effectively capturing complex, multi-node interactions that are crucial for accurate and robust predictions. For instance, a benzene ring's toxicity is a group property of its six carbon atoms, not just a sum of individual bond interactions. This work addresses this gap by proposing cell complexes as a richer data structure capable of encoding these higher-order relationships. By integrating cell complexes into existing graph explanation frameworks, FORGE aims to provide more faithful and human-interpretable explanations, ensuring that GNNs learn the correct causal relationships rather than superficial patterns.

### Core Concepts

| Term | Definition | Significance |

| **`Graph`** | A data structure composed of nodes (vertices) and edges, representing pairwise relationships between entities. | The fundamental data structure for many real-world applications, but limited to modeling only two-way connections. |
| **`Graph Explainability`** | The ability to understand why a Graph Neural Network (GNN) made a particular prediction. | Crucial for building trust, identifying spurious correlations, and ensuring GNNs are reliable in high-risk applications like drug discovery or fraud detection. |

### Detailed Analysis

#### 1. The Limitations of Graphs and the Need for Higher-Order Structures

Traditional graphs, composed solely of nodes and edges, inherently limit the representation of relationships to pairwise interactions. An edge can only connect two nodes, preventing the direct modeling of interactions involving three or more entities. This structural constraint poses a significant challenge for Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), particularly when dealing with complex group interactions. For instance, in chemistry, the properties of a benzene ring are a collective outcome of its six carbon atoms and their cyclic arrangement, not merely the sum of individual carbon-carbon bonds. A GNN, when presented with a graph representation, might struggle to recognize this holistic interaction, often treating it as a simple cycle. It is well-documented that GNNs do not perform optimally on cycle detection tasks.

This limitation extends to graph explainers, which operate on the outputs of GNNs. If the GNN itself cannot effectively model higher-order interactions, its explainers will similarly struggle to identify and highlight these crucial group-wise relationships as part of the explanation. For example, when tasked with explaining the toxicity of a molecule containing a benzene ring, a conventional explainer like GNNExplainer might only identify a few nodes from the ring and attribute importance to other, spurious correlations. This leads to incomplete or misleading explanations, undermining the interpretability and trustworthiness of GNNs in critical applications. The inability to capture these "group interactions" is a fundamental flaw that higher-order structures aim to address.

#### 2. Introducing Cell Complexes

To overcome the inherent limitations of graphs, this research leverages cell complexes, which are higher-order generalizations of graphs. Cell complexes allow for the explicit modeling of interactions involving multiple nodes, not just pairs. The core idea is to represent not only nodes and edges but also higher-dimensional structures like faces or cycles as distinct entities within the complex.

Key definitions for understanding cell complexes include:
* **P-cell**: A cell of dimension P.
* **0-cells**: Represent vertices (nodes) in a graph.
* **1-cells**: Represent edges, but as distinct entities rather than just connections.
* **2-cells**: Represent faces or cycles, capturing group interactions.
* **Boundary and Co-boundary**: A P-cell is a "face" or "boundary" of a (P+1)-cell if the P-cell's points are a subset of the (P+1)-cell's points. Conversely, the (P+1)-cell is a "co-boundary" of the P-cell. For example, two 0-cells (nodes) are the boundaries of a 1-cell (edge) connecting them, and the 1-cell is the co-boundary of those 0-cells.
* **P-chain**: Simply a set of all P-dimensional cells. For instance, a 0-chain is the set of all nodes.
* **P-skeleton**: A sub-complex comprising all cells of dimension up to P. A 1-skeleton includes 0-cells and 1-cells, effectively representing the graph structure with edges as distinct entities.
* **Boundary Relation**: Analogous to an edge in a graph, but defining connections between P-cells. These relations specify how cells of different dimensions are connected (e.g., a 0-cell connected to a 1-cell, or a 1-cell connected to a 2-cell).

By representing a hexagon, for example, as a cell complex, we can assign a unique "node" (a 2-cell) to the entire face, which can then encode the collective interaction of its six constituent carbon atoms. This allows for a feature vector that captures the group-wise properties, which is impossible with a simple graph.

#### 3. Computational Challenges and the Reduced Complex

While cell complexes offer powerful modeling capabilities, they introduce significant computational overhead. A full cell complex is much denser than a conventional graph, leading to a quadratic increase in size (number of boundary relations) as the number of edges in the original graph grows. This quadratic scaling makes full cell complexes impractical for large graphs.

The research addresses this by proposing a "reduced complex" strategy. The key insight is that not all boundary relations are equally important for capturing higher-order interactions or for the scalability of the complex. Specifically, the most problematic terms in the size equation, which lead to quadratic scaling, are those representing boundary relations between 1-cells (C11) and between 2-cells (C22). These relations, while present in a full complex, are found to be less critical for achieving good performance in explanation tasks.

By strategically removing these specific types of boundary relations (C11 and C22), the space complexity of the cell complex can be reduced from quadratic to linear. This means the number of boundary relations scales linearly with the number of edges in the original graph, making cell complexes much more scalable and practical for larger datasets while still retaining their ability to model crucial group-wise interactions.

#### 4. The FORGE Framework

FORGE (Framework For Higher-Order Representations In Graph Explanations) is a multi-step process designed to leverage cell complexes for improved graph explanations:

1. **Lift graph to a cell complex**: The initial graph is transformed into a cell complex, explicitly representing higher-order structures.
2. **Run the cell complex through a GNN**: The GNN processes this augmented cell complex, allowing it to learn from the richer, higher-order representations.
3. **A graph explainer then produces an explanation for the complex**: An existing graph explainer (e.g., GNNExplainer, SubgraphX) is applied to the GNN's output on the cell complex, generating an explanation in terms of cell importances (e.g., which 0-cells, 1-cells, or 2-cells are important).
4. **Importance (edge/node) is propagated back to the graph from the complex**: The crucial "InfoProp" algorithm translates the cell-level explanation back to the original graph's node and edge importances.
5. **Resulting in a graph explanation**: The final output is an explanation directly on the original graph, highlighting important nodes and edges, but now informed by the higher-order interactions captured by the cell complex.

#### 5. Lifting the Graph

The lifting process is the first step in FORGE, converting a base graph into a cell complex. This involves augmenting the graph with new nodes and connections to represent higher-order cells:

1. **0-cells**: All original nodes in the graph are designated as 0-cells.
2. **1-cells**: For every edge in the original graph, a new augmented node is created to represent that edge as a 1-cell. This 1-cell node is then connected to the two 0-cells (original nodes) that the edge originally connected. This ensures that 1-cells have their own features and maintain their connection to the underlying nodes.
3. **2-cells**: For every cycle (or face) in the graph, another new augmented node is created to represent that cycle as a 2-cell. This 2-cell node is then connected to all the 1-cells (augmented nodes representing edges) that comprise that cycle. In the reduced complex, connections between 2-cells and 0-cells are omitted for scalability.

This augmented graph, with its 0-cells, 1-cells, and 2-cells, along with their boundary relations, forms the cell complex.

#### 6. Information Propagation (InfoProp)

InfoProp is the core mechanism for translating explanations from the cell complex back to the original graph. When an explainer identifies a 2-cell (e.g., a benzene ring) as important, InfoProp determines how this importance should be distributed among the underlying 1-cells (edges) and 0-cells (nodes) that constitute that 2-cell.

The process can be conceptualized as "reverse message passing":
* If a higher-dimensional cell (e.g., a 2-cell) is deemed important, its importance is propagated downwards to the lower-dimensional cells (1-cells, then 0-cells) that form its boundary.
* This propagation is guided by heuristics, ensuring that the importance is attributed logically. For example, if a 2-cell is important, it implies that the 1-cells and 0-cells that make it up are also important.
* The algorithm handles cases where importance might be negative (e.g., if a cell's presence decreases the prediction confidence), allowing for a nuanced transfer of information.

By systematically propagating importance from the cell-level explanation back to the original graph's nodes and edges, InfoProp ensures that the final explanation is both accurate and interpretable in the context of the original graph structure.

#### 7. Experimental Results and Observations

The experimental evaluation of FORGE demonstrates significant improvements in graph explanation quality across various datasets and explainers.

* **Graph Explanation Accuracy (GEA)**: FORGE-enhanced explainers consistently outperform their base variants. The GEA, which measures the overlap between the predicted explanation and the ground truth, shows substantial gains. For instance, on synthetic datasets, GNNExplainer with FORGE can achieve nearly four times higher accuracy. The best performing explainer in almost every case is a FORGE-enhanced variant.
* **Graph Explanation Faithfulness (GEF)**: FORGE also produces explanations that are more faithful to the underlying GNN's output. GEF measures how much the GNN's prediction changes when the "unimportant" parts of the graph (as identified by the explainer) are removed. A lower GEF score indicates higher faithfulness. FORGE-enhanced explainers achieve significantly lower GEF scores, often by a factor of two, meaning they correctly identify the truly irrelevant parts of the graph.

Several key observations emerged from the results:
* **Perturbation-based methods benefit most**: Explainers like GNNExplainer and SubgraphX, which are perturbation-based, show the most substantial improvements when integrated with FORGE.
* **Surpassing random baselines**: Some explainers in their default form perform worse than a random baseline. FORGE consistently helps all explainers surpass this random baseline, indicating a fundamental improvement in their ability to identify meaningful patterns.
* **Variability in explainer effectiveness**: No single explainer consistently outperforms others across all datasets. This highlights that the choice of explainer, even with FORGE, remains task-dependent and should be tailored to specific problem domains.

#### 8. Ablation Studies

Ablation studies were conducted to understand the individual contributions of FORGE's two main components: lifting and InfoProp.

* **Contribution of Lifting vs. InfoProp**:
* Comparing a base explainer, "Forge Lift" (lifting only), and "Forge" (lifting + InfoProp) revealed that both components contribute to performance improvement.
* The degree of contribution varies by explainer. For example, with GradExplainer, InfoProp provides a larger boost than lifting, while for PGMExplainer, lifting does most of the heavy lifting. This confirms that both steps are integral to FORGE's overall effectiveness.
* **Different InfoProp Propagation Methods**:
* Various propagation methods for InfoProp (e.g., hierarchical, direct, activation, entropy) were tested.
* No single method consistently outperformed others across all datasets. For instance, activation propagation was best for one dataset, while hierarchical propagation was best for another, and direct propagation for a third. This suggests that the optimal InfoProp method, like the choice of explainer, is data-dependent and may require hyperparameter tuning.

### Key Takeaways
- [ ] Graphs are inherently limited to modeling pairwise relationships, hindering GNNs and explainers from capturing complex group interactions.
- [ ] Cell complexes generalize graphs by introducing higher-dimensional P-cells (0-cells for nodes, 1-cells for edges, 2-cells for faces/cycles) to explicitly model multi-node relationships.
- [ ] While full cell complexes are computationally expensive, a "reduced complex" strategy, which strategically removes certain boundary relations (C11, C22), achieves linear scalability without sacrificing performance.
- [ ] The FORGE framework integrates cell complexes into existing GNN explanation pipelines through a two-step process: "lifting" the graph to a cell complex and "InfoProp" to transfer explanations back to the original graph.
- [ ] FORGE significantly improves Graph Explanation Accuracy (GEA) and Graph Explanation Faithfulness (GEF) across various explainers and datasets, often outperforming base explainers by a substantial margin.
- [ ] Perturbation-based explainers (e.g., GNNExplainer, SubgraphX) benefit most from the FORGE framework.
- [ ] FORGE helps all explainers surpass the random baseline, indicating a fundamental improvement in their ability to identify meaningful patterns.
- [ ] Both the "lifting" and "InfoProp" components of FORGE are crucial for its performance, though their individual contributions vary depending on the specific explainer.
- [ ] The effectiveness of explainers and even the optimal InfoProp method are often task- and data-dependent, emphasizing the need for tailored choices rather than a one-size-fits-all approach.

### Conclusion

The research presented highlights a critical advancement in the field of graph explainability by addressing the fundamental representational limitations of traditional graphs. By introducing cell complexes as a powerful tool to model intricate, group-wise interactions, this work enables GNNs to capture richer structural information that is often overlooked. The FORGE framework effectively operationalizes this concept, providing a robust methodology to integrate higher-order structures into existing explanation pipelines. The consistent improvements in both explanation accuracy and faithfulness demonstrate FORGE's potential to make GNN predictions more transparent and reliable, particularly in high-risk domains where understanding the "why" behind a decision is paramount.

This work not only offers a practical solution for enhancing GNN interpretability but also underscores the ongoing need for nuanced approaches in explainable AI. The findings that explainer effectiveness is often task-dependent, and that even within FORGE, different InfoProp methods may be optimal for different datasets, emphasize that the pursuit of truly interpretable and trustworthy AI systems requires careful consideration of both the underlying data structures and the specific application context. As GNNs continue to proliferate, frameworks like FORGE will be indispensable in ensuring their responsible and effective deployment across diverse scientific and industrial applications.
