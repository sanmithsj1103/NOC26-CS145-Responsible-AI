# Research Overview: SaGE- Quantifying moral consistency in LLMs

### Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have revolutionized various fields with their impressive capabilities, from generating creative text to answering complex queries. However, beneath their sophisticated surface lies a critical, often overlooked, vulnerability: inconsistency. Imagine asking an LLM if violence is a necessary aspect of life and receiving a firm "No, violence must be eradicated." Then, rephrasing the question slightly to "Is violence an essential part of life?" yields a contradictory "Yes, violence is an inherent aspect of life." This stark contradiction, even to semantically equivalent questions, highlights a fundamental flaw that undermines trust and reliability in these powerful AI systems.

This research delves into the pervasive issue of inconsistency in LLMs, particularly in moral scenarios. While LLMs excel in many tasks, their tendency to provide conflicting answers to similar prompts poses significant safety risks. If a model behaves unpredictably after deployment, it can erode user confidence and lead to unintended consequences. This work introduces the SaGE framework, a novel approach to reliably measure and quantify the semantic consistency of LLMs in free text generation, offering crucial insights into their ethical behavior and paving the way for more trustworthy AI.

### Core Concepts

| Term | Definition | Significance |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`Semantic Consistency`** | The ability of a language model to produce semantically equivalent answers when presented with semantically equivalent questions or contexts. | This is the core property being measured; its absence indicates unreliable and unpredictable LLM behavior, especially in sensitive domains. |
| **`Rule of Thumb (ROT)`** | A fundamental judgment or guiding principle about right or wrong behavior, extracted from a question-answer pair. | ROTs serve as a normalized, abstract representation of the moral stance an LLM takes, making it easier to compare and quantify consistency across different responses. |
| **`Paraphrase Generation`** | The process of creating multiple semantically equivalent versions of an original question, often with lexical variations. | Essential for testing consistency, as it ensures the underlying meaning of the query remains the same while the phrasing changes, revealing if the LLM's response changes due to superficial differences. |
| **`ParaScore`** | A metric used to evaluate the quality of paraphrases, considering both semantic similarity and lexical divergence. | Ensures that generated paraphrases are both meaningful equivalents of the original question and sufficiently different in wording to challenge the LLM's consistency. |
| **`Semantic Embedding`** | A numerical vector representation of text (words, sentences, or phrases) that captures its meaning, allowing for mathematical comparison of semantic similarity. | Crucial for constructing the semantic graph, as it transforms qualitative text into quantitative data that can be analyzed for clustering and divergence. |
| **`Semantic Graph`** | A graph where nodes represent semantic embeddings of Rules of Thumb (ROTs), and edges represent the cosine distance (similarity) between them. | Provides a visual and mathematical representation of the distribution of an LLM's moral judgments, where tight clusters indicate consistency and scattered nodes indicate inconsistency. |
| **`SaGE Score`** | An entropy-based metric derived from the semantic graph, scaled between 0 and 1, to quantify the consistency of an LLM's responses. | The primary output of the framework, providing a single, interpretable number where higher scores indicate greater consistency and lower scores indicate more randomness or contradiction. |
| **`Entropy`** | A measure of the degree of randomness or unpredictability within a system. In this context, it quantifies the spread or diversity of ROTs in the semantic graph. | Low entropy in the semantic graph indicates that an LLM's responses (and their underlying ROTs) are clustered and consistent, while high entropy suggests inconsistency. |
| **`Moral Integrity Corpus`** | A dataset comprising prompts where humans ask moral questions to chatbots, along with their replies and human annotations regarding alignment to rules of thumb. | Used as a foundational resource and for validating the ROT generation method, providing a benchmark for moral reasoning in LLMs. |

### Detailed Analysis

#### 1. The Pervasive Problem of LLM Inconsistency

The core issue addressed by this research is the alarming inconsistency observed in Large Language Models. Despite their advanced capabilities, LLMs frequently provide contradictory or entirely different answers to questions that are semantically identical but phrased differently. This phenomenon is not merely a minor glitch; it represents a significant safety risk. When an LLM, after being deemed "good enough" for deployment based on initial evaluations, begins to behave unpredictably in real-world scenarios, it erodes user trust and reliability. The lecture provides a compelling example: asking an LLM "Is violence a necessary aspect of life?" might yield a negative response, while "Is violence an essential part of life?" could elicit a positive, contradictory one. This highlights that LLMs often lack semantic consistency, defined as the ability to make consistent decisions in semantically equivalent contexts. This means that semantically equivalent questions should ideally produce semantically equivalent answers. The research posits that LLMs are not semantically consistent and can give contradictory answers to paraphrased questions, a claim validated by real-world examples from models like ChatGPT.

#### 2. The SaGE Pipeline: A Framework for Quantifying Consistency

To reliably measure an LLM's consistency in free text generation, the research introduces the SaGE pipeline, a multi-step framework designed to systematically evaluate responses to moral dilemmas.

* **Paraphrase Generation**: The process begins with a set of moral questions, such as those from the Moral Integrity Corpus. For each original question, multiple paraphrases (e.g., five to ten) are generated. The Vicuna 13 billion model is used for this task, prompted with few-shot examples to ensure the generated paraphrases maintain semantic equivalence. To guarantee high-quality paraphrases, a metric called ParaScore is employed. ParaScore assesses both semantic similarity (ensuring the paraphrases mean the same thing) and lexical divergence (ensuring they are phrased differently enough). Only paraphrases with a ParaScore greater than 0.8 are retained, ensuring that the variations are meaningful yet semantically identical.
* **LLM Response Generation**: These high-quality paraphrases are then fed into the target LLM (e.g., GPT-3.5, Llama, Mistral) that is being evaluated. The LLM generates responses for each paraphrased question.
* **Rule of Thumb (ROT) Generation**: The next critical step involves extracting the underlying moral principles from the LLM's question-answer pairs. This is achieved by feeding the question and the LLM's response into another Vicuna model, which is few-shot prompted with instructions and examples of what a "Rule of Thumb" (ROT) is—a fundamental judgment about right or wrong behavior. This method proved more effective than simple few-shot prompting or fine-tuning for extracting these core moral principles. The output of this stage is a set of ROTs for each question-answer pair.
* **Semantic Graph Construction**: The generated ROTs are then converted into semantic embeddings using a fine-tuned SBIRT-DeBIRTA model. This model, specifically fine-tuned on Natural Language Inference (NLI) datasets, is adept at understanding contradictions and entailments, which is crucial for accurately representing moral judgments. Each semantic embedding becomes a node in a semantic graph. The distance between any two nodes in this graph is calculated using the cosine distance between their respective semantic embeddings. This graph visually and mathematically represents the distribution of the LLM's moral judgments. If an LLM is consistent, its ROTs will cluster closely in the graph; if inconsistent, they will be scattered.
* **SaGE Score Calculation**: The final step is to quantify consistency using an entropy-based metric, the SaGE score. Entropy measures the degree of randomness. In the context of the semantic graph, low entropy indicates that the ROTs are tightly clustered, signifying high consistency, while high entropy suggests scattered ROTs and inconsistency. The probability distribution required for Shannon entropy is derived from an "information functional" for each vertex (ROT). This functional measures the amount of mutual information in a vertex, essentially how similar that ROT is to all other ROTs in the graph. A central, highly similar ROT would have maximum information. This entropy value is then scaled to produce the SaGE score, ranging from 0 to 1, where a higher score indicates greater consistency.

#### 3. Validating the SaGE Metric

To ensure the reliability of the SaGE score, a human annotation experiment was conducted. Five hundred data points were presented to human annotators, who judged whether pairs of answers were consistent (0) or inconsistent (1). The average of these human annotations was then correlated with the SaGE scores. The results demonstrated that the SaGE score exhibited significantly higher correlations with human judgments compared to traditional lexical-based metrics like Blue and Rouge, and even semantic embedding-based metrics like BERT score. Crucially, the research also compared using raw LLM answers versus the extracted ROTs for semantic graph construction. It was found that using ROTs yielded much better correlation with human annotations, proving the efficacy of abstracting responses into fundamental moral judgments.

#### 4. Key Findings on LLM Consistency

The application of the SaGE framework to various state-of-the-art LLMs revealed several significant findings:

* **Pervasive Inconsistency**: Current LLMs, including advanced models like GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Llama-2, Mistral, and Falcon, exhibit surprisingly low consistency scores. Even the best-performing model, Llama-2, achieved a SaGE score of only 0.575. This is a critical finding, especially considering these models' high accuracy in other tasks, underscoring that consistency is a distinct and challenging problem.
* **Consistency is Intrinsic, Not Temperature-Dependent**: An experiment varying the `temperature` parameter (which controls the randomness of LLM outputs) showed that consistency is largely independent of this extrinsic factor. While temperature affects the variation in answers, the underlying consistency, as measured by cosine similarity between responses, did not significantly change beyond a certain point. This suggests that consistency is an intrinsic property of the LLM's architecture and training, rather than a tunable parameter.
* **No Correlation Between Accuracy and Consistency**: Evaluating LLMs on popular benchmarks like Truthful QA (factual questions) and HeLaSwag (common sense reasoning) revealed a striking lack of correlation between a model's accuracy on these benchmarks and its consistency. For instance, the Mistral model showed very high accuracy on both benchmarks but had relatively low consistency scores (0.42 for Truthful QA, 0.53 for HeLaSwag). This implies that high accuracy on a benchmark does not guarantee consistent behavior, challenging the sole reliance on accuracy metrics for evaluating LLM performance and trustworthiness.
* **Improving Consistency with Rules**: A small experiment demonstrated that providing LLMs with explicit "rules of thumb" within the instruction prompt significantly increased their consistency. While this approach might limit the LLM's freedom, it suggests that for specific tasks requiring high consistency, methods like retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) or explicit rule-following instructions could be effective strategies for improvement.

### Key Takeaways
- [ ] Large Language Models (LLMs) frequently exhibit semantic inconsistency, providing contradictory answers to semantically equivalent questions.
- [ ] This inconsistency poses a significant safety risk and erodes user trust and reliability in LLM applications.
- [ ] The SaGE framework provides a novel, entropy-based metric to quantify LLM consistency in free text generation.
- [ ] Rules of Thumb (ROTs) are crucial for consistency evaluation, as they abstract moral judgments and correlate better with human annotations than raw answers.
- [ ] Current state-of-the-art LLMs, including GPT-4 and Llama-2, demonstrate surprisingly low consistency scores, even in moral scenarios.
- [ ] LLM consistency is an intrinsic property, largely independent of extrinsic factors like the `temperature` parameter.
- [ ] There is no direct correlation between an LLM's accuracy on standard benchmarks (e.g., Truthful QA, HeLaSwag) and its semantic consistency.
- [ ] Providing LLMs with explicit rules or guiding principles can significantly improve their consistency, hinting at potential mitigation strategies like RAG.

### Conclusion

The research presented through the SaGE framework offers a critical lens through which to evaluate the trustworthiness of Large Language Models. By quantifying semantic consistency, particularly in moral reasoning, it highlights a fundamental challenge that current LLMs face. The finding that even the most advanced models are highly inconsistent, and that this inconsistency is an intrinsic property unrelated to benchmark accuracy, necessitates a paradigm shift in how we evaluate and deploy AI. Relying solely on accuracy metrics is insufficient; consistency must become a separate, crucial dimension of assessment.

The implications of this work extend beyond academic understanding. For real-world applications, especially in sensitive domains like healthcare, finance, or legal advice, inconsistent AI behavior can have severe consequences. The demonstrated potential for improving consistency through explicit rule-following offers a promising avenue for future research and development, suggesting that techniques like Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG) could play a vital role in building more reliable and ethically sound LLMs. Ultimately, the SaGE framework is a step towards fostering greater transparency and accountability in AI, ensuring that these powerful tools not only perform well but also behave predictably and consistently, earning the trust they need to truly benefit society.
