# Unlearning

### Introduction

The rapid advancement of Artificial Intelligence, particularly in large language and vision models, has led to an unprecedented increase in model parameters and the sheer volume of training data. These foundational models are often trained on vast amounts of publicly available internet data, leading to remarkable capabilities but also significant challenges. As models grow in complexity and data consumption, the need for "unlearning" becomes critical. This module delves into machine and graph unlearning, exploring why it's a burgeoning and essential field in responsible AI. It addresses the complex problem of how to remove specific, undesirable information from a trained model without the prohibitive cost and effort of retraining the entire system from scratch, a capability increasingly demanded by legal frameworks and ethical considerations.

### Core Concepts

| Term | Definition | Significance |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`Machine Unlearning`** | The process of removing the influence of specific training data from a trained machine learning model. | Enables models to "forget" information, crucial for privacy, security, and ethical AI. |
| **`Right to be Forgotten`** | A legal principle, notably from GDPR, granting individuals the right to request the erasure of their personal data. | A primary legal driver for the development and implementation of machine unlearning techniques. |
| **`Access Revocation`** | The act of removing a model's ability to access or utilize specific private or copyrighted information it was previously trained on. | Addresses concerns about data ownership and intellectual property within AI models. |
| **`Model Correction/Editing`** | Post-training modifications to a model to alter its behavior, often to remove biases, toxicity, or update stale knowledge. | A practical approach to mitigate risks and improve model safety without full retraining. |
| **`Exact Unlearning`** | A method where the data to be unlearned is precisely known, allowing for targeted removal with strong guarantees. | Offers high confidence that specific data points have been completely removed from the model's influence. |
| **`Approximate Unlearning`** | A method used when the data to be unlearned is underspecified or vague, aiming for a practical level of removal. | Provides a more flexible approach when precise data identification for unlearning is challenging. |
| **`Sharding (SISA Training)`** | A technique where large training data is divided into smaller, isolated shards, and models are trained on these segments. | Facilitates efficient exact unlearning by allowing only relevant constituent models to be retrained. |
| **`Stale Knowledge`** | Outdated or incorrect information that a model may have learned from its training data. | Requires unlearning to ensure models provide current and accurate information. |
| **`Toxic/Unsafe Content`** | Harmful, offensive, or dangerous information that a model might generate or perpetuate if not unlearned. | Essential to remove for ethical AI and to prevent the spread of harmful narratives. |
| **`Misinformation`** | False or inaccurate information that a model has learned and might disseminate. | Unlearning is vital to combat the spread of fake news and ensure factual integrity. |

### Detailed Analysis

#### 1. The Growing Need for Unlearning in Modern AI
The landscape of modern AI is characterized by an exponential increase in model parameters and the sheer volume of data used for training. Models like large language models and vision models now boast billions of parameters and are often trained on publicly available data scraped from the internet. While this enables impressive capabilities, it introduces significant challenges. Training these models is incredibly expensive, costing tens to hundreds of millions of dollars. This high cost makes frequent retraining impractical.

Furthermore, publicly available data can contain undesirable information that models might learn, such as:
- **Private data:** Personal information like phone numbers or dates of birth.
- **Stale knowledge:** Outdated facts (e.g., a former director's name).
- **Copyrighted materials:** Content protected by intellectual property rights.
- **Toxic and unsafe content:** Harmful or offensive language, often found on platforms like Reddit.
- **Dangerous capabilities:** Information that could enable the creation of harmful items (e.g., bio-chemical weapons).
- **Misinformation:** False or misleading information.

The core need for unlearning is the ability to edit or remove these types of undesirable data influences from a trained model *without* incurring the massive cost and time of retraining the entire model from scratch. This capability is crucial for maintaining model integrity, safety, and compliance.

#### 2. Defining Machine Unlearning: A Formal Perspective
Machine unlearning can be broadly described as the process of removing the influences of specific training data from a trained model. More formally, the goal is to produce an "unlearned model" that is either equivalent to, or at least behaves like, a model that was originally trained on the same dataset *minus* the information intended to be unlearned. This means the unlearned model should perform all original tasks effectively, except for those related to the forgotten data.

The precise definition of unlearning can vary based on several factors:
- **ML Task:** The type of machine learning task (e.g., binary classification, language modeling, summarization) influences what "forgetting" entails.
- **Data to be Unlearned:** This could range from specific images or news articles to complex knowledge like how to construct a particular device.
- **Algorithm Used:** The approach to unlearning, whether it involves heuristic fine-tuning or more direct deletion of model components.
- **Goals:** The ultimate objective, such as ensuring user privacy or removing harmful capabilities.

#### 3. Legal and Ethical Motivations: The Right to be Forgotten
A significant driver for the development of machine unlearning is legal frameworks, particularly the European Union's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and its "Right to Erasure," commonly known as the "Right to be Forgotten." This right empowers individuals (data subjects) to request that data controllers (e.g., Facebook, Google) erase their personal data without undue delay under specific conditions. These conditions include:
- The personal data is no longer necessary for the purpose for which it was collected.
- The data subject withdraws consent for processing.
- The data subject objects to the processing, and there are no overriding legitimate grounds for continued processing.

The challenge for AI models is that once data is consumed and integrated into a model's parameters, it's not easily "returned" or "deleted" in a straightforward manner. As illustrated by the analogy of "Ram ate Lakshman's laddu," once data is "eaten" by a model and used for decision-making, simply "puking" it back is not feasible. The model has made connections and inferences that are hard to disentangle. This difficulty in proving data deletion from complex ML models has spurred intense interest in machine unlearning.

#### 4. Approaches to Unlearning: Access Revocation and Model Correction
Two primary ways to conceptualize machine unlearning are:

**Access Revocation:** This approach focuses on removing the model's ability to access or utilize private or copyrighted information. It's about taking the information *away* from the model's learned representations. A major challenge here is proving that the data has indeed been revoked. If a user requests their data be removed, how can the model owner demonstrate that the model no longer retains any influence from that data?
- **Periodic Retraining:** One proposed solution is to periodically retrain models, incorporating all unlearning requests received over a specific timeline. This allows for batch processing of deletion requests.
- **Policy and Cost:** Policymakers might mandate periodic retraining, but the immense cost of training large models (e.g., $100 million) raises questions about the economic viability of frequent retraining for model owners like OpenAI. This necessitates a balance between regulatory demands and practical implementation.

**Model Correction or Editing:** This is treated as a post-training risk mitigation mechanism. After a model is built, changes are made to its internal state to correct undesirable behaviors or content. This is often driven by a desire for models to be more usable and desirable, rather than a strict necessity for formal guarantees of data removal. Examples include:
- Removing toxic biases.
- Updating stale information.
- Correcting factual errors.
While formal proofs of unlearning are difficult, the goal is to deploy "scientifically safe" or "sufficiently safe" models, such as chatbots, even if they still present some challenges. Benchmarks are being developed to evaluate the effectiveness of these model editing and correction techniques.

#### 5. Forms of Unlearning: Exact vs. Approximate
Unlearning can be categorized into different forms based on the precision of the data to be removed and the guarantees provided:

**Exact Unlearning:** This form applies when the data to be unlearned is precisely known. The goal is to ensure that the unlearned model is distributionally identical to a model that was never trained on the specific data points in question.
- **SISA (Sharded, Isolated, Sliced, Aggregated) Training:** A method for achieving exact unlearning.
- The original training data (T) is divided into multiple **shards**.
- Each shard is further divided into **slices**.
- Constituent models are trained on each shard incrementally, saving parameters before new slices are added.
- If specific data (D) needs to be unlearned, and it resides within a particular shard, only the constituent model trained on that shard needs to be retrained.
- Retraining starts from the parameter values saved *before* the slice containing the data to be unlearned was included.
- **Benefits:** This significantly reduces retraining costs. Instead of retraining a $100 million model, one might only retrain a component that cost $100,000.
- **Proof by Design:** The design ensures that the unlearned data never contributed to other constituent models, providing inherent proof of its removal.
- **Interpretability:** Because smaller models are involved, it's easier to understand how specific data points influenced the model's performance.

**Approximate Unlearning:** This form is used when the data to be unlearned is underspecified or vague. The aim is to achieve a practical level of unlearning without necessarily providing the strong guarantees of exact unlearning.
- **Unlearning via Differential Privacy (DP):** Differential privacy techniques can be integrated to provide privacy guarantees, which inherently contribute to unlearning by limiting the influence of individual data points.
- **Empirical Unlearning:** This category can further distinguish between cases where data to be unlearned is precisely known (similar to exact unlearning but potentially with weaker guarantees) and cases where it is underspecified (e.g., "remove all knowledge about X," without knowing all specific data points contributing to X).

### Key Takeaways
- [ ] Modern AI models' vast scale and reliance on public data necessitate unlearning for safety and compliance.
- [ ] Unlearning aims to remove specific data influences from a trained model without costly full retraining.
- [ ] The "Right to be Forgotten" (GDPR) is a key legal driver for machine unlearning.
- [ ] Proving that data has been effectively unlearned from a complex model is a significant challenge.
- [ ] Access revocation focuses on removing a model's ability to use private/copyrighted data.
- [ ] Model correction/editing is a post-training mitigation for biases, toxicity, and stale knowledge.
- [ ] Exact unlearning, often using SISA training, targets precisely known data for removal with strong guarantees.
- [ ] Approximate unlearning addresses scenarios where the data to be forgotten is underspecified or vague.
- [ ] SISA training reduces unlearning costs by only retraining relevant constituent model shards.
- [ ] The definition and implementation of unlearning depend on the ML task, data, algorithm, and specific goals.

### Conclusion
Machine unlearning is rapidly emerging as a cornerstone of responsible and safe AI development. As AI models become more pervasive and powerful, their ability to "forget" specific information is not just a technical challenge but a legal and ethical imperative. From safeguarding user privacy under regulations like GDPR to mitigating the spread of misinformation and toxic content, unlearning provides a crucial mechanism for maintaining control over AI's learned knowledge. The ongoing research into exact and approximate unlearning methods, such as SISA training, highlights the industry's commitment to building adaptable models that can evolve and correct themselves without prohibitive costs. This field will continue to shape how we interact with AI, ensuring that these intelligent systems remain aligned with human values and societal expectations.
