### Core Concepts

| Term | Definition | Significance |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`Graph Convolutional Network (GCN)`** | A type of neural network that generalizes convolutional operations to graph-structured data, allowing it to learn features from nodes and their neighbors. | Essential for processing graph data, GCNs form the backbone of many graph unlearning methods by learning node representations. |
| **`Node Representation`** | A vector embedding that captures the features and contextual information of a specific node within a graph. | Fundamental for tasks like node classification or link prediction; unlearning often involves modifying these representations. |
| **`Adjacency Matrix`** | A square matrix representing a finite graph, where elements indicate whether pairs of nodes are connected by edges. | Crucial for defining graph structure and connectivity, directly influencing how information propagates in GCNs and MPNNs. |
| **`Degree Matrix`** | A diagonal matrix where each diagonal element specifies the number of edges connected to a node (its degree). | Used in GCN formulations to normalize node features and ensure stable learning across nodes with varying connectivity. |
| **`Message Passing Neural Network (MPNN)`** | A general framework for graph neural networks where nodes iteratively exchange and aggregate information (messages) from their neighbors. | Provides an intuitive model for how information flows and is processed in graphs, directly relevant to how unlearning impacts connected nodes. |
| **`Node Unlearning`** | The process of removing a specific node and all its associated edges and features from a trained graph model. | Addresses privacy requests where an entire entity needs to be forgotten from the model's knowledge base. |
| **`Edge Unlearning`** | The process of removing a specific connection (edge) between two nodes from a trained graph model. | Important for scenarios where a relationship between entities needs to be forgotten, without necessarily removing the entities themselves. |
| **`Node Feature Unlearning`** | The process of removing specific attributes or features associated with a node, while the node itself and its connections remain. | Allows for granular privacy control, enabling the forgetting of sensitive attributes without altering the graph's topology. |
| **`Over-forgetting`** | A phenomenon in unlearning where the removal of specific information inadvertently degrades the performance on the retained data. | A significant challenge in graph unlearning, as interconnected data makes it difficult to isolate the impact of forgetting. |
| **`Continued Learning`** | The ability of a model to adapt and update its knowledge incrementally after unlearning, without retraining from scratch. | A key practical challenge in graph unlearning, as many methods struggle to efficiently incorporate new data or updates post-unlearning. |

### Detailed Analysis

#### 1. Applications and Foundations of Graph Processing

Graphs are powerful data structures that model relationships between entities, finding extensive applications across diverse domains. The lecture highlights several key areas where graphs are indispensable:

* **Healthcare Networks**: Connecting hospitals, doctors, and patients to model disease spread, treatment efficacy, or referral patterns.
* **Biological Networks**: Representing interactions between genes, proteins, or neurons, crucial for understanding complex biological processes.
* **Adversarial Settings**: Modeling networks of individuals to identify malicious actors or detect fraudulent activities.
* **Financial Networks**: Analyzing transactions between banks, users, and market participants to detect fraud or predict market trends.
* **Transportation Networks**: Mapping roads, public transport routes, and logistics for navigation and optimization (e.g., Google Maps).
* **Collaborative Environments**: Representing connections between individuals, groups, or institutions working on projects.
* **Network of Devices**: Interconnecting IoT devices, sensors, and smart infrastructure for data collection and decision-making (e.g., smart city lighting).

To process these complex graph structures, specialized neural networks are employed. The **Graph Convolutional Network (GCN)**, introduced by Thomas and Max, is a cornerstone. GCNs are understood as a generalization of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) to graph-structured data. The core idea is that a GCN layer defines a first-order approximation of a localized spectral filter on graphs, allowing it to learn features by aggregating information from a node's immediate neighbors.

The formal expression of a GCN layer involves several key components:
* `H`: A matrix representing node representations.
* `X`: A matrix of node features.
* `σ`: An activation function, such as ReLU.
* `Ã`: An adjacency matrix with added self-loops (e.g., a user liking their own post).
* `D̃`: A graph degree matrix, also with added self-loops.
* `θ`: A matrix of trainable parameters.

This formulation highlights that GCNs learn by considering a node's own features, its connections (adjacency matrix), and the degree of its neighbors, all processed through trainable parameters and an activation function.

#### 2. Message Passing Neural Networks (MPNNs)

Building on the intuition of GCNs, **Message Passing Neural Networks (MPNNs)** provide a more general framework for understanding how information propagates through a graph. The fundamental idea behind MPNNs is that a node's representation is updated by aggregating "messages" received from its neighbors. This concept is crucial because in a graph, a node's features and information are heavily influenced by its immediate surroundings.

An MPNN operates on a graph `G = (V, E)`, where `V` is the set of nodes and `E` is the set of edges.
* `N(u)`: Represents the set of neighbors of a node `u`.
* `X_u`: Denotes the features of node `u` (e.g., height, weight, college).
* `E_uv`: Represents the features of the edge connecting nodes `u` and `v`.

The message passing process can be formally expressed using differentiable functions `φ` (message function) and `γ` (update function), and a permutation-invariant aggregation operator (e.g., sum, mean, max). In essence, each node gathers messages from its neighbors, aggregates these messages, and then updates its own representation based on this aggregated information and its previous state. This iterative process allows nodes to learn rich, context-aware representations that incorporate information from their local graph neighborhood. These updated node representations can then be used for various downstream tasks, such as:
* **Node Classification**: Classifying a node into a category (e.g., "good node" or "bad node").
* **Edge Prediction**: Predicting the likelihood of a connection forming between two nodes (e.g., recommending friends in a social network).
* **Graph Classification**: Classifying an entire graph based on its structure and node features.

#### 3. Types of Graph Unlearning Operations

Within the domain of graph unlearning, three primary types of removal requests are identified, each targeting a different aspect of the graph structure:

* **Node Feature Unlearning**: This involves removing specific attributes or features associated with a particular node, without deleting the node itself or its connections. For example, unlearning the fact that "Professor PKA works at IIIT Hyderabad" while PKA remains a node in the graph. This is useful for granular privacy requests concerning specific data points.
* **Node Unlearning**: This is the most comprehensive form, where an entire node, along with all its associated features and all edges connected to it, is removed from the graph. For instance, completely removing "Professor PKA" from the graph. This addresses requests for complete erasure of an entity.
* **Edge Unlearning**: This involves removing a specific connection or relationship between two nodes, while both nodes remain in the graph. An example would be unlearning the connection between "Professor PKA" and "Professor X," implying they are no longer connected in the model's representation. This is relevant for forgetting specific interactions or relationships.

These three types of unlearning requests form the basis for developing specific graph unlearning algorithms, as illustrated by a framework that depicts the original graph, the unlearning request, and the resulting unlearned model.

#### 4. Methods for Graph Unlearning

Several methods have been proposed to address the challenges of graph unlearning, each with its own approach and limitations:

* **Graph Eraser**: This method primarily focuses on **node unlearning**. It does not support feature unlearning or edge unlearning. Its core idea often involves sharding, which can be computationally intensive and complex to implement.
* **Guide**: Guide is designed for **node unlearning** but does not handle edge or feature unlearning. It optimizes partitioning and graph aggregation methods, also frequently relying on sharding, which presents similar implementation difficulties.
* **Projector**: This method specializes in **feature unlearning**. It works by projecting weight parameters such that features intended for forgetting become irrelevant. A limitation is that it often works best with linear GNNs, which might restrict its applicability to more complex models.
* **Graph Editor**: This is a more versatile method, capable of performing **node, edge, and feature unlearning**. It leverages a closed-form solution to efficiently remove the effect of deleted nodes first, and then updates the effects on neighboring nodes. This sequential approach aims to manage the cascading impacts of deletions.
* **GNN Delete**: This method supports **node, edge, and feature unlearning**. It introduces shared weight matrices across nodes and employs a layer-wise deletion operator to update the model. A significant disadvantage is its difficulty in handling continued learning requests, meaning it's hard to incrementally update the model after unlearning.
* **Megu (Mutual Evolution Graph Unlearning)**: Megu is a more recent approach that also supports **node, edge, and feature unlearning**. It uses an adaptive high-level influence neighborhood selection for custom loss functions, allowing it to intelligently decide which neighbors to consider for message passing and feature updates. As a newer method, its long-term disadvantages are still being explored.

These methods highlight the diverse strategies employed, from direct parameter manipulation to structural re-evaluation, each balancing effectiveness with computational cost and flexibility.

#### 5. Challenges and Future Directions in Graph Unlearning

Despite the advancements, graph unlearning faces several significant challenges that are active areas of research:

* **Impact on Retained Set Performance**: Most unlearning methods prioritize forgetting the requested data but may inadvertently impact the performance of the *retained* data. This is particularly problematic in graphs due to their interconnected nature, where removing one piece of information can subtly alter the representations of many others.
* **Over-forgetting**: A common issue is that models tend to "over-forget" information, meaning they remove more than what was strictly requested, leading to a loss of valuable knowledge. This is a critical concern, especially for graph methods where dependencies are complex.
* **Lack of Focus on Continued Learning**: Many existing methods struggle with continued learning. After an unlearning operation, it is often difficult to incrementally update the model with new data without retraining it entirely from scratch. This limits their applicability in dynamic, real-world scenarios where models need to be continuously updated.
* **Real-world Applicability and Evaluation**: A key question remains whether these methods are truly forgetting information effectively and if their performance degradation on retained data is acceptable for real-world deployment. Developing robust metrics and testing methodologies to properly evaluate the efficacy and impact of graph unlearning is an ongoing challenge.
* **Scalability**: Graph unlearning, especially for large-scale graphs, can be computationally expensive. Methods involving sharding or extensive re-computation can become impractical for massive networks.

These challenges underscore the need for more efficient, precise, and adaptable graph unlearning techniques that can maintain model utility while strictly adhering to forgetting requests. This area represents a fertile ground for future research, inviting innovative solutions to these complex problems.
