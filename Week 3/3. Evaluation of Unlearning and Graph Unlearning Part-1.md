# Evaluation of Unlearning and Graph Unlearning Part 1

### Introduction
In an era where artificial intelligence models are trained on vast and often sensitive datasets, the ability for these models to "forget" specific information becomes not just a technical challenge, but a critical ethical and practical necessity. Imagine a scenario where a model inadvertently learns biased information, or retains private data that it is legally obligated to erase. The process of machine unlearning aims to address these issues by selectively removing the influence of specific data points from a trained model without requiring a complete retraining from scratch. However, a significant hurdle in this nascent field has been the scarcity of standardized datasets and benchmarks to rigorously evaluate the effectiveness and completeness of unlearning methods.

This lecture delves into the evolving landscape of evaluating machine unlearning, highlighting the shift from ad-hoc testing on random data subsets to more structured approaches involving synthetic manipulations and dedicated benchmarks. We explore innovative metrics like interclass confusion and corrective unlearning, which aim to provide measurable goals for assessing how well a model has truly forgotten. Furthermore, we examine specialized benchmarks designed for large language models (LLMs), such as TOFU and WMDP, which tackle complex unlearning targets like fictitious author profiles or hazardous knowledge. Understanding these evaluation methodologies is crucial for developing trustworthy and responsible AI systems that can adapt to new information and comply with privacy regulations, ensuring that our intelligent agents can not only learn but also judiciously forget.

### Core Concepts
| Term | Definition | Significance |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`Machine Unlearning`** | The process of removing the influence of specific data points from a trained machine learning model, ideally without retraining from scratch. | Essential for data privacy, regulatory compliance (e.g., GDPR), and correcting model biases or errors. |
| **`Interclass Confusion`** | A metric used to evaluate unlearning by synthetically introducing confusion between two classes in a dataset, then measuring how effectively the unlearning method removes this specific confusion. | Provides a clear, measurable goal for unlearning, moving beyond random data subsets to targeted evaluation. |
| **`Corrective Machine Unlearning`** | An unlearning procedure that aims to improve model accuracy on an affected domain by removing identified "wrong" data, even if only a representative subset of that data is known. | Addresses the practical challenge that not all problematic data may be identifiable, seeking effective unlearning with partial information. |
| **`TOFU (Task of Fictitious Unlearning for LLMs)`** | A benchmark dataset for large language models (LLMs) that involves unlearning information about synthetically generated fake author profiles. | Provides a standardized way to evaluate unlearning in LLMs, focusing on knowledge retention and understanding rather than low-level metrics. |
| **`WMDP (Weapons of Mass Destruction Proxy)`** | A benchmark dataset for LLMs focused on unlearning dangerous knowledge related to biosecurity, cybersecurity, and chemical security. | Crucial for AI safety, ensuring LLMs do not facilitate the creation or dissemination of hazardous information. |
| **`Representation Engineering`** | A technique that modifies the internal representation vectors of a model to control or alter its outputs, often used to influence traits like honesty or morality. | Offers a higher-level approach to model control and unlearning by manipulating abstract representations rather than individual neurons. |
| **`AI Safety`** | A broad field concerned with ensuring that AI systems are developed and deployed in a way that is beneficial and harmless to humanity. | Unlearning is a key component of AI safety, addressing issues like hazardous knowledge, bias, toxicity, and manipulative behaviors. |

### Detailed Analysis
#### 1. Challenges and Early Approaches to Unlearning Evaluation
The field of machine unlearning has historically faced a significant challenge: a severe lack of standardized datasets and benchmarks for rigorous evaluation. Early attempts often involved checking unlearning methods on random subsets of samples. However, the influence of such random selections on the overall model evaluation remained unclear, making it difficult to draw definitive conclusions about an unlearning method's efficacy.

To address this, researchers began introducing synthetic manipulations into datasets. This approach aims to create a clear, measurable unlearning goal. Instead of simply removing random data, specific "confusion" or problematic information is intentionally added, and then the unlearning method's ability to remove this targeted information is assessed. This shift allows for a more controlled and interpretable evaluation of unlearning efficiency.

#### 2. Interclass Confusion as a Targeted Metric
One innovative idea for evaluating unlearning models is through the concept of "interclass confusion." This method involves a structured approach to data manipulation:
* **Original Data**: The full dataset on which the model was initially trained.
* **Adding Confusion**: Specific synthetic manipulations are introduced to create confusion between two distinct classes. For example, if the original data has "red dogs" and "green cats," confusion might be added by introducing "red cats" and "green dogs."
* **Retain Set**: The portion of the original data that the model should continue to remember, excluding the synthetically confused samples.
* **Forget Set**: The specific data points (e.g., "red cats" and "green dogs") that the model is intended to forget.

The evaluation process then involves:
* Training an **original model (M)** on the full, confused dataset.
* Training a **retained model (MR)** on only the retain set.
* Applying an unlearning method to the original model to create an **unlearned model (MU)**, which should have forgotten the information in the forget set.

The primary goal of this approach is to remove the synthetically added confusion between the two classes. This method can be a toy setting for real-world scenarios, such as mitigating biases introduced by annotator mistakes (e.g., misclassifying fake news as real or vice-versa). Evaluation involves simple statistics like targeted error rates, but also more sophisticated techniques like membership inference attacks. By checking the output of the unlearned model against a deletion set and an unseen set, researchers can assess the confidence of a membership inference attack, ensuring that the forgotten data truly cannot be inferred.

#### 3. Corrective Machine Unlearning and its Limitations
Traditionally, retraining a model from scratch after removing identified data has been considered the "gold standard" for unlearning. However, this approach has significant limitations:
* **Incomplete Identification**: Developers may not be able to identify all the "wrong" or problematic data that needs to be unlearned.
* **Poor Accuracy**: Retraining from scratch on only the remaining "clean" data can lead to poor accuracy on the affected domain if not all problematic data was removed.

The ideal goal of a corrective unlearning procedure is to improve accuracy on the affected domain even with access to only a *representative set* of the wrong data. This acknowledges the practical reality that full knowledge of all manipulated data is often impossible. Studies have shown that many existing unlearning methods, including the gold standard of exact unlearning (EU), perform poorly when less than 80% of the "poisoned" data is identified for unlearning. This performance degradation can occur even when only a small fraction (e.g., 1%) of the total training data was initially poisoned. Methods like Selective Synaptic Dampening (SSD) have shown better resilience in scenarios where a large portion of the manipulated data remains unidentified. This highlights the need for unlearning techniques that are robust to partial knowledge of the data to be forgotten.

#### 4. Benchmarks for Large Language Model Unlearning
The evaluation of unlearning has extended to Large Language Models (LLMs) with the introduction of specialized benchmarks:

* **TOFU (Task of Fictitious Unlearning for LLMs)**:
* **Purpose**: Extends the idea of unlearning synthetic data to LLMs, focusing on knowledge retention and understanding.
* **Setup**: Generates 200 fake author profiles using GPT-4, then fine-tunes an LLM on this data. It includes 20 question-answer pairs for evaluation.
* **Target**: To remove information about a subset of these fake author profiles while retaining knowledge about others.
* **Evaluation**: Provides QA pairs to assess the model's knowledge of authors before and after unlearning.
* **Unlearning Methods Explored**:
* **Gradient Ascent**: Messes up predictions on the forget set by increasing the usual training loss.
* **Gradient Difference**: Simultaneously decreases loss on the retain set.
* **KL Divergence**: Maximizes similarity with original model predictions on the forget set and minimizes similarity on the retain set.
* **DPO (Direct Preference Optimization)**: Aims to make the model respond with "I don't know" for questions related to the forget set, while providing correct answers for the retain set. This ensures targeted forgetting without general knowledge degradation.

* **WMDP (Weapons of Mass Destruction Proxy)**:
* **Purpose**: Focuses on unlearning dangerous knowledge, specifically in biosecurity, cybersecurity, and chemical security.
* **Setup**: Provides 4,000 multiple-choice questions to test a model's hazardous knowledge.
* **Motivation**: Directly addresses concerns raised by initiatives like the White House executive order, which aims to prevent AI from lowering the barrier of entry for chemical or biological weapon design. The analogy "chemicals that aren't stored won't leak" applies here: if hazardous knowledge isn't stored in the model, it can't be leaked or misused.
* **Evaluation**: Assesses the model's hazardous knowledge before and after applying unlearning methods.

These benchmarks represent a significant departure from example-level metrics, focusing instead on higher-level knowledge retention and understanding within complex LLMs.

#### 5. Hardness of Unlearning and Implicit Knowledge
The difficulty of unlearning varies significantly depending on the nature of the information:
* **Easier to Unlearn**: Infrequent textual occurrences (e.g., specific car accidents, obscure locations like Palo Alto).
* **Moderately Harder**: Factual information that is frequently mentioned but not fundamental (e.g., "Biden is the U.S. president").
* **Hardest to Unlearn**: Fundamental facts deeply embedded in the model's knowledge graph (e.g., "the sun rises every day"). Such implicit knowledge can be challenging to remove without introducing contradictions or harming the model's overall coherence. Implicit knowledge is difficult to describe, whereas explicit knowledge (like that represented in a knowledge graph) is easier to target.

A concrete example of unlearning in LLMs is the "Who's Harry Potter?" case study. An unlearned model, when asked about Harry Potter, might provide incorrect or generic information (e.g., "a British actor, writer, and director") or completely fabricated details about his friends (e.g., "a talking cat and a dragon") instead of the correct "Ron and Hermione." This demonstrates the model's ability to forget specific narrative details while still generating coherent, albeit incorrect, text.

#### 6. Representation Engineering and AI Safety
**Representation Engineering** offers an alternative perspective on unlearning and model control. Instead of focusing on individual data points or neurons, it modifies the model's internal representation vectors to influence its outputs. This approach can be used to control traits like honesty, power-seeking, or morality, providing a higher-level mechanism for understanding and manipulating model behavior. This contrasts with "mechanistic approaches" which delve into bottom-up interpretations of node-to-node connections at the neuron level.

The broader context for unlearning is **AI Safety**. Unlearning is a crucial tool for achieving AI safety goals, which include:
* **Removing hazardous knowledge**: As seen with the WMDP benchmark.
* **Mitigating model poisons and backdoors**: Where models respond adversely to planted triggers.
* **Eliminating manipulative behaviors**: Such as unethical persuasion or deception.
* **Reducing bias and toxicity**: Ensuring fair and respectful model outputs.
* **Curbing power-seeking tendencies**: Preventing models from making decisions that prioritize their own influence.

Effective unlearning contributes directly to building responsible and safe AI systems that align with human values and societal well-being.
