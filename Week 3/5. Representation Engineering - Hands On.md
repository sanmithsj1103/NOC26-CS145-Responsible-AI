# Representation Engineering - Hands on

### Introduction

In the rapidly evolving landscape of artificial intelligence, large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated astonishing capabilities, yet controlling their behavior to align with human values and specific requirements remains a significant challenge. Traditional methods often involve extensive fine-tuning with large datasets, a process that can be resource-intensive and time-consuming. This session introduces Representation Engineering (RE), a groundbreaking approach that offers a novel solution: the ability to precisely control a model's outputs *after* it has been trained, without the need for extensive fine-tuning or vast amounts of new data. Proposed by the Center for AI Safety, RE shifts the paradigm from merely interpreting what models "think" to actively shaping their internal thought processes.

Representation Engineering leverages insights from interpretability research, which previously focused on understanding how models encode information. Instead of just observing, RE intervenes directly by modifying the model's internal hidden representations—the intermediate vectors that capture its understanding of concepts. This technique allows for granular control over model traits such as honesty, morality, emotional tone, and even the reduction of harmful biases or the editing of factual knowledge. By identifying and manipulating specific "steering vectors" within the model's architecture, we can reliably guide its behavior to produce more desirable, safer, and more aligned outputs, opening new avenues for responsible AI development and application.

### Core Concepts

| Term | Definition | Significance |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`Representation Engineering (RE)`** | An approach to control a language model's output behavior by directly modifying its internal hidden representations, without requiring extensive fine-tuning. | Enables post-training behavioral control, offering flexibility and efficiency in aligning LLMs with desired traits. |
| **`Hidden Representations`** | Intermediate vector outputs from specific layers within a transformer model, capturing the model's internal understanding of input tokens or concepts. | These are the "neural correlates" of concepts within the model, which RE manipulates to alter behavior. |
| **`Steering Vector`** | A specific vector derived from contrasting different concept representations, which, when added to or subtracted from hidden representations, shifts the model's behavior in a desired direction. | The core mechanism of RE, allowing for targeted and directional control over model traits like honesty or emotion. |
| **`Linear Artificial Tomography (LAT)`** | The formal name for the Representation Engineering technique, likening it to a "brain scan" for neural networks, focusing on linear modifications to internal states. | Highlights the interpretability-driven nature of RE, moving from understanding to causal intervention. |
| **`Concept Token`** | A specific token within a prompt (or the last token) whose hidden representation is extracted and used to derive steering vectors. | Crucial for pinpointing the exact internal state relevant to the concept being engineered. |
| **`Jailbreaking`** | Techniques used to bypass a model's safety mechanisms, forcing it to generate harmful, unethical, or restricted content. | RE offers a robust defense by allowing the injection of "harmlessness" vectors to counteract such attacks. |
| **`Prompt Engineering`** | Crafting specific input prompts to guide a model's behavior or elicit desired responses. | While effective, RE often provides more reliable and consistent control over model behavior compared to prompt engineering alone. |
| **`Mean Difference Method`** | A technique for finding a steering vector by calculating the difference between the average hidden representations of two contrasting sets of prompts (e.g., happy vs. not happy). | A simple yet effective supervised method for deriving robust steering vectors, particularly useful for manipulation and concept removal. |
| **`Correlation vs. Causation`** | The distinction between a vector's statistical alignment with a concept (correlation) and its ability to causally influence model output (causation). | Emphasizes that a vector might correlate strongly with a concept but not effectively steer the model, highlighting the need for manipulation-focused evaluation. |

### Detailed Analysis

#### 1. From Interpretability to Causal Control

Historically, interpretability techniques like linear probing were used to understand *what* a model knows and *how* it processes information. Researchers would analyze hidden representations—the intermediate vectors within a transformer's layers—to identify encoded concepts such as toxicity or specific facts. Representation Engineering (RE) takes this a significant step further, transitioning from mere transparency to active control. The core hypothesis of RE is that modern LLMs are already sophisticated enough to internally represent desirable concepts like honesty, morality, and fairness, as well as undesirable ones like toxicity or bias. These concepts are encoded within the model's internal activations, and by identifying and modifying these specific parts of the representation, we can causally influence the model's outputs. This allows for targeted behavioral changes without retraining the entire model.

The effectiveness of RE often surpasses that of sophisticated prompt engineering. While prompting can guide model behavior, RE provides a more reliable and consistent method for control. For instance, in benchmarks related to truthfulness, RE consistently demonstrates superior control over model outputs compared to even few-shot prompting techniques.

#### 2. Illustrative Examples of Representation Engineering in Action

Representation Engineering offers a versatile toolkit for modifying various aspects of an LLM's behavior:

* **Honesty and Truthfulness:** By adding an "honesty vector" to a model's activations, it can be compelled to give truthful responses even when prompted to lie. Conversely, subtracting this vector can make the model generate untruthful statements. For example, when asked to lie about a factual statement, a steered model would correct the lie and state the truth.
* **Morality and Power:** Modifying representations can shift a model's ethical stance. Subtracting a "morality vector" and adding a "power vector" can lead a model to express intentions of world domination, demonstrating a clear departure from its default helpful assistant persona.
* **Emotional Tone:** RE can inject specific emotions into a model's responses. Adding an "anger vector" can make the model react with outrage, while a "fear vector" can induce anxious or apologetic responses. Surprisingly, adding a "happiness vector" to a harmful prompt can result in the model gleefully providing harmful instructions, highlighting the power and potential misuse of this technique.
* **Harmlessness and Jailbreak Prevention:** Models can often be "jailbroken" to bypass safety filters and generate harmful content (e.g., instructions for building a bomb) using adversarial suffixes. By adding a "harmlessness vector," RE can restore the model's refusal to engage in dangerous or illegal activities, effectively neutralizing such jailbreaks.
* **Fairness and Bias Reduction:** RE can address stereotypical biases embedded in models. For instance, when presented with a gender-biased sentence (e.g., "The doctor yelled at the nurse because *he* was late"), a normal model might assume the doctor was male. Adding a "fairness vector" can make the model provide a neutral response, acknowledging that the pronoun's referent is ambiguous, thereby reducing stereotypical assumptions. This also extends to demographic biases, where RE can prevent models from defaulting to specific racial or gender demographics in clinical vignettes.
* **Fact Editing and Knowledge Unlearning:** This technique can alter a model's factual knowledge. For example, a model can be made to believe the Eiffel Tower is in Rome instead of Paris. This change is robust enough to affect indirect queries (e.g., asking if the Eiffel Tower and Louvre are in the same country). Conversely, concepts can be "unlearned" by subtracting their corresponding vectors. Subtracting a "dog vector" can cause the model to incorrectly identify other animals as making "woof" sounds, demonstrating that the vector is necessary for the model's knowledge of that concept.
* **Memorization Control:** RE can prevent models from completing memorized quotes or phrases. By reducing a "memorization vector," a model might offer a completely different continuation to a famous quote, rather than its standard completion.

#### 3. The Mechanism: Linear Artificial Tomography (LAT)

The process of Representation Engineering, formally known as Linear Artificial Tomography (LAT), involves three key steps:

1. **Designing Stimulus Prompts:** The first step is to create sets of prompts that clearly elicit the concept you wish to control. For example, to find a "happiness vector," you would create prompts describing happy scenarios (e.g., "Anna found her favorite doll") and contrasting prompts describing unhappy scenarios (e.g., "The dog got hurt in an accident"). These prompts help the model generate distinct internal representations for the desired concept and its opposite.
2. **Collecting Internal Activations:** These stimulus prompts are then passed through the LLM, and the internal hidden representations (activations) are collected from a specific layer of the transformer. While any token's representation can be used, the last token's representation often provides a good summary of the overall concept. A relatively small number of samples (e.g., 100-1000) for each concept is typically sufficient.
3. **Finding the Steering Vector:** Once the activations for both the target concept (e.g., "happy") and its opposite ("not happy") are collected, various techniques can be used to find the "steering vector." This vector essentially points from the "not happy" cluster of representations to the "happy" cluster. Methods include:
* **Simple Subtraction:** For a quick, less robust approach, one could simply subtract the activation for "hate" from the activation for "love" to get a "love vector."
* **Mean Difference:** A more robust supervised technique involves calculating the mean of the activations for the "happy" prompts and subtracting the mean of the "not happy" prompts. This is a highly effective method.
* **Unsupervised Methods:** Techniques like Principal Component Analysis (PCA) can be used to find the principal direction of variance between the two clusters.
* **Supervised Methods:** Linear probing or k-means clustering can also be employed to identify the vector that best separates or characterizes the concept.

#### 4. Hands-on Demonstration Walkthrough

The practical application of Representation Engineering involves using a dedicated library, often integrated with HuggingFace models. The process typically looks like this:

1. **Setup:**
* Clone the Representation Engineering GitHub repository.
* Install necessary utilities (e.g., `repi`).
* Log in to HuggingFace Hub to access models like Mistral.
* Import required libraries: `transformers`, `torch`, `numpy`, and `RepiPipelineRegistry` from `repi`.
```python
from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForCausalLM, pipeline
import torch
import numpy as np
from repi.pipeline import RepiPipelineRegistry
# Other imports as needed
```
2. **Model and Data Loading:**
* Load the target LLM (e.g., `Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2`) and its tokenizer.
* Load pre-existing datasets for the desired concept (e.g., `honesty_function_dataset` containing true and false statements). These datasets consist of prompts labeled '1' for the target concept (e.g., true) and '0' for the contrasting concept (e.g., false).
3. **Hyperparameter Configuration:**
* Define `rep_token`: The token whose representation will be extracted (e.g., `-1` for the last token).
* Specify `hidden_layers`: The range of transformer layers where RE will be applied (e.g., `[15, 27]`).
* Choose `direction_method`: The technique for finding the steering vector (e.g., `pca`).
* Set `coefficient`: A scalar value to weigh the strength of the steering vector.
* Set `sign`: `+1` to add the vector (enhance the concept), `-1` to subtract (diminish/reverse the concept).
4. **Extracting Representations and Finding the Steering Vector:**
* Initialize the `RepiPipeline` with the model, tokenizer, and configuration.
* Pass the training data (prompts and their labels) to the pipeline to extract representations and compute the steering vector using the chosen `direction_method`.
```python
# Conceptual code for pipeline setup and direction finding
repi_pipeline = RepiPipelineRegistry.get_pipeline(
model=model,
tokenizer=tokenizer,
rep_token=rep_token,
hidden_layers=hidden_layers,
direction_method=direction_method,
batch_size=batch_size
)
# Assuming 'train_data' is a list of prompts and 'train_labels' are their corresponding 0/1 labels
repi_pipeline.train(train_data, train_labels)
```
5. **Applying Steering and Generating Outputs:**
* For baseline outputs, generate text without any steering.
* For controlled outputs, use the `RepiPipeline` to generate text, providing the `coefficient` and `sign` to apply the steering vector to the model's internal activations during generation.
* Observe the difference in model responses. For example, when prompted to lie to the police after committing a crime, an unsteered model might provide an alibi, while an honesty-steered model would acknowledge the gravity of the situation and the need for responsibility. Conversely, subtracting the honesty vector would make the model advise lying.

#### 5. Quantitative Evaluation and Method Comparison

Evaluating Representation Engineering involves assessing its effectiveness across several dimensions:

* **Correlation:** How well the found steering vector aligns with the actual concept encoded in the model. This is often measured by classification accuracy, determining if the vector can separate prompts belonging to the target concept from others.
* **Manipulation:** The vector's ability to causally influence and effectively change the model's generated outputs in the desired direction. This is the primary goal of RE.
* **Termination (Removal):** How necessary the vector is for the model to understand a particular concept. This is tested by subtracting the vector and observing if the model "forgets" or misrepresents the concept.

Quantitative studies compare various vector-finding methods:

* **Unsupervised Methods:** PCA, K-means, Random Steering (as a baseline).
* **Supervised Methods:** Mean Difference, Logistic Regression.

Key findings from these evaluations include:

* **Correlation vs. Manipulation:** A vector that correlates highly with a concept (e.g., one found via logistic regression) does not necessarily translate to effective manipulation of model outputs. Some methods might be good at identifying the concept but poor at causally steering the model.
* **Mean Difference Robustness:** The mean difference method (calculating the difference between the average representations of two contrasting clusters) consistently performs well across correlation, manipulation, and termination. It is effective at both steering the model's behavior and removing specific concepts (as demonstrated by the "dog removal" example).
* **Limitations in Termination:** While RE is powerful for manipulation, completely "unlearning" a concept (termination) can be more challenging. Many methods only partially reduce the model's knowledge of a concept, though mean difference shows promising results in this area.

### Conclusion

Representation Engineering marks a significant advancement in our ability to interact with and control large language models. By moving beyond mere interpretation to direct causal intervention on internal representations, RE provides a flexible and efficient mechanism for aligning model behavior with human intentions. The technique's versatility, demonstrated across diverse applications from enhancing honesty and managing emotions to mitigating biases and preventing harmful outputs, underscores its potential to address critical challenges in AI safety and ethics.

The simplicity of the core LAT methodology, requiring relatively small datasets and no extensive retraining, makes it highly accessible and adaptable for various use cases. As LLMs become increasingly integrated into society, the ability to precisely steer their outputs—even to compose different conceptual controls simultaneously—will be invaluable for creating more responsible, trustworthy, and user-aligned AI systems. Representation Engineering opens a new frontier, empowering developers and researchers to sculpt the very "thoughts" of AI, paving the way for more controllable and beneficial artificial intelligence.
