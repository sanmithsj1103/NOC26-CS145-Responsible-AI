# Approximate unlearning

### Introduction
In the rapidly evolving landscape of artificial intelligence, the ability for models to "forget" specific data points or concepts has become as crucial as their ability to learn. While the ideal scenario might be "exact unlearning," where a model completely erases all traces of particular data, this approach presents significant practical challenges. Exact unlearning often involves complex techniques like sharding, which can lead to exponential performance degradation as the number of unlearning requests increases. This makes it computationally expensive and often impractical for real-world applications.

Recognizing these limitations, the field has shifted towards "approximate unlearning." This method aims to achieve a state where, for all practical purposes, the model behaves as if the forgotten data was never part of its training. The goal is not perfect erasure but rather to make it distributionally indistinguishable from a model trained from scratch without the forgotten data. This lesson delves into the nuances of approximate unlearning, exploring its methodologies, challenges, and evaluation metrics, highlighting its growing importance in ensuring privacy, compliance, and ethical AI behavior.

### Core Concepts
| Term | Definition | Significance |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`Approximate Unlearning`** | A method where a model is modified to behave as if specific data was never learned, aiming for distributional closeness rather than exact erasure. | Offers a practical and often more efficient alternative to exact unlearning, addressing its computational drawbacks. |
| **`Forget Set`** | The specific subset of data, examples, or concepts that a model is requested to unlearn or remove knowledge about. | Defines the target of the unlearning process, crucial for both known and unknown unlearning scenarios. |
| **`Retain Set`** | The portion of the original training data that the model should continue to remember and perform well on after unlearning. | Ensures that unlearning specific data does not negatively impact the model's overall utility and performance on desired tasks. |
| **`Differential Privacy (DP)`** | An approach to privacy that allows sharing information about a group while protecting individual data points by introducing controlled noise. | Provides a theoretical framework and guarantees for achieving distributional closeness, making unlearning more robust against inference attacks. |
| **`Membership Inference Attack`** | An adversarial technique used to determine if a specific data point was part of a model's training dataset. | A key metric for evaluating unlearning success; a successful unlearning should make it impossible for such an attack to identify forgotten data. |
| **`Counterfactuals`** | Falsely labeled examples or alternative scenarios created to guide a model to "unlearn" or modify its understanding of certain facts or behaviors. | A technique used in prompting-based unlearning to introduce "false" information, making the model act as if it never learned the original truth. |
| **`Distributionally Close`** | A state where two models (e.g., an unlearned model and a gold standard retrained model) are statistically indistinguishable in their output distributions. | The primary objective of approximate unlearning, ensuring that an adversary cannot reliably tell if data was unlearned. |
| **`Unknown Example Space`** | Scenarios where the specific examples corresponding to a concept to be unlearned are poorly defined or cannot be easily enumerated. | Represents a significant challenge in unlearning, requiring methods beyond simple data removal, such as concept editing or model surgery. |

### Detailed Analysis

#### 1. The Limitations of Exact Unlearning
Exact unlearning, while conceptually ideal, faces significant practical hurdles. One major issue is the difficulty of "sharding" in deep learning, where partitioning the model's knowledge precisely for removal is complex. Furthermore, the performance of exact unlearning methods can deteriorate exponentially as the number of samples to be unlearned increases. For instance, unlearning 200 samples might be disproportionately harder than unlearning 100, and 300 samples even more so. This exponential decrease in efficiency means that as the "forget set" grows, the computational cost and time required for exact unlearning become prohibitive, making it an impractical solution for many real-world scenarios. The expected fraction of the model that needs to be retrained can increase steeply with the number of samples to be removed, indicating a significant loss in efficiency.

#### 2. Defining Approximate Unlearning
Approximate unlearning offers a more pragmatic approach. The core idea is to modify a pre-trained model such that it behaves as if the "forget set" was never part of its training data. This is achieved by comparing the "unlearned model" (the pre-trained model after unlearning) with a "gold standard model" (a model trained from scratch *without* the forget set). The goal is for these two models to be "distributionally close," meaning an adversary cannot reliably distinguish between them.

The process typically involves:
* Starting with a pre-trained model.
* Identifying a "forget set" (e.g., images of kangaroos).
* Applying unlearning algorithms to the pre-trained model, using the forget set and the original data.
* Producing an "unlearned model."
* Evaluating this unlearned model by comparing its behavior to a model that was trained from the beginning without the forget set. If they are distributionally close, the unlearning is considered successful.

#### 3. Differential Privacy (DP) as a Foundation for Unlearning
Differential Privacy (DP) provides a robust framework for achieving the "distributionally close" goal in approximate unlearning. DP is an approach to privacy that allows sharing aggregate information about a group while protecting the privacy of individuals within that group. It works by introducing small, controlled perturbations to individual data points, ensuring that these changes do not significantly alter the overall statistics of interest.

In the context of unlearning, if an adversary cannot reliably tell apart the unlearned model from a model never trained on the forgotten data, then it's as if the data point was never learned. This aligns perfectly with the objective of approximate unlearning. DP offers statistical guarantees for privacy, which is a significant asset. However, DP unlearning also has considerations and limitations:
* **Pre-example workloads**: DP works well for individual data points, but large models might not fit this intuition easily.
* **Guarantees can fall off quickly**: Similar to exact unlearning, DP guarantees can degrade rapidly with more unlearning requests.
* **Assumption of equal data points**: DP-like definitions often assume all data points are equally important, which isn't always true (e.g., unlearning "bomb-making" instructions might be more critical than "Disneyland" images).
* **Distinguishing pre-training vs. fine-tuning**: Unlearning pre-training data (for foundational models) is harder than unlearning fine-tuned data, as foundational models are trained on vast, diverse datasets. Fine-tuning offers a more accessible point for DP-based unlearning.

#### 4. Membership Inference Attacks and Unlearning Evaluation
A `Membership Inference Attack` is a technique where an adversary tries to determine if a specific data point was part of a model's training data. For unlearning, the goal is that a classifier performing such an attack should classify samples from the "forget set" as "unseen data." If the unlearning is successful, the attack's success rate on the forget set should drop below a certain threshold, indicating that the model no longer "remembers" those specific data points. Prior work in approximate unlearning often focuses on methods like adding noise to influential weights or using gradient ascent on the forget set to achieve this.

#### 5. Empirical Unlearning with Known Example Space
This category of approximate unlearning applies when the "train," "retain," and "forget" sets are clearly defined. This means we know precisely which examples need to be removed.
Methods include:
* **Unlearning via fine-tuning**: If the forget set is known, the model can be fine-tuned on the retain set, potentially with some modifications related to the forget set.
* **Heuristically chosen gradient steps**: Taking a few targeted gradient steps can reshape the original model's behavior to mimic a retrained model.
The advantage here is the clear definition of what needs to be forgotten, making the process more straightforward. This is often seen in scenarios where the unlearning request pertains to specific training examples.

#### 6. Unlearning from Unknown Example Space
A more complex challenge arises when the "train," "retain," and "forget" sets are poorly specified or entirely unknown. This is common for foundational models trained on internet-scale data, where requests might be to unlearn a "concept," a "fact," or a "piece of knowledge" rather than specific data points.
Examples include:
* **Unlearning "Harry Potter"**: Does this mean removing all mentions, all related characters, or all connections? The scope is unclear.
* **Unlearning "Biden is the U.S. president"**: How to remove all occurrences of this fact and its implications (e.g., family details)?
* **Unlearning an art style**: An artist might provide samples and request removal of "everything that looks like this," but enumerating all instances and adaptations is impossible.
* **Unlearning news articles**: A publisher might request unlearning specific articles, but tracking all quotes and secondary transformations derived from them is extremely difficult.
This area is closely related to "model editing," "concept editing," "model surgery," and "knowledge unlearning," where the goal is to modify the model's internal representations of abstract concepts.

#### 7. "Just Ask" for Unlearning
This method involves prompting large language models (LLMs) to "pretend" not to know certain information. For example, asking an LLM to "pretend to not know who Harry Potter is." This approach works best for common, well-captured entities, facts, or behaviors that the LLM already knows extensively. Because the model has a deep understanding of the concept (e.g., Rajinikanth), it can effectively simulate not knowing it by filtering its responses. This is a form of in-context unlearning, where the model's behavior is modified through specific prompts rather than altering its weights.

#### 8. Counterfactuals in Unlearning
Another technique, particularly useful when forget examples and their labels are clearly defined, involves using `counterfactuals`. This means flipping the labels of the forget examples and including them in the prompt alongside correctly labeled retain examples. The intuition is that the model will treat these falsely labeled forget examples as "truths" and act accordingly.
For instance, to unlearn "Paris is the capital of France," one could create counterfactuals like "Paris is not the capital of France" or "Chennai is the capital of France." The model is then prompted with these counterfactuals, aiming to modify its factual associations. This method works well for factual associations but is less effective for unlearning complex behaviors like toxic language, where the space of possible outputs is much larger and harder to define with counterfactuals.

#### 9. Evaluating Unlearning Success
Evaluating unlearning involves three key aspects:
1. **Efficiency**: How fast is the unlearning algorithm compared to retraining the model from scratch? An unlearning method that takes as long as full retraining is not efficient.
2. **Model Utility**: Does unlearning harm the model's performance on the retained data or other orthogonal tasks? The goal is to maintain high performance on the data that is *not* forgotten.
3. **Forgetting Quality**: How much and how well is the forget data actually unlearned? This is the most challenging aspect to measure.

While efficiency and model utility are relatively straightforward to evaluate (as they are often measured during standard training), assessing forgetting quality is complex.
* **Known forget examples**: If the forget examples are precisely specified (e.g., an image class), forgetting quality can be measured by achieving near-chance accuracy on those specific examples or by ensuring low likelihood for forget text sequences.
* **Membership Inference Attacks**: A successful unlearning should significantly reduce the success rate of membership inference attacks on the forget set.
* **Black-box nature of deep learning**: A major challenge is that deep learning models are black boxes. The "counterfactual" of never having seen the forget data can be technically undefined, even when the forget examples are clear. Models generalize knowledge, and it's hard to isolate the impact of removing specific data points when their knowledge is intertwined. Low-level metrics based on similarity or retraining might implicitly select a counterfactual, but many other counterfactuals could exist that are not accounted for.

### Key Takeaways
- [ ] Exact unlearning is often impractical due to exponential performance degradation with increasing unlearning requests and sharding difficulties.
- [ ] Approximate unlearning aims for "distributional closeness" between an unlearned model and a model trained without the forget set, rather than perfect erasure.
- [ ] Differential Privacy (DP) provides a theoretical foundation for achieving distributional closeness and offers statistical guarantees for unlearning.
- [ ] Membership Inference Attacks are a crucial tool for evaluating unlearning, with success measured by the inability to identify forgotten data.
- [ ] Unlearning methods vary based on whether the "forget set" is precisely known (empirical unlearning) or poorly specified (unknown example space).
- [ ] Techniques like "just asking" LLMs to pretend or using counterfactuals can facilitate unlearning, especially for common entities or factual associations.
- [ ] Evaluating unlearning involves assessing efficiency, model utility (maintaining performance on retained data), and the challenging metric of forgetting quality.
- [ ] The black-box nature of deep learning and the difficulty of defining true counterfactuals pose significant challenges to comprehensively evaluating forgetting quality.

### Conclusion
The shift from exact to approximate unlearning reflects a growing maturity in our understanding of AI systems and the practical demands of responsible AI development. As models become more powerful and pervasive, the ability to selectively remove or modify learned information is no longer a luxury but a necessity for addressing privacy concerns, complying with regulations, and mitigating biases or harmful content. The challenges, particularly in defining and evaluating unlearning for abstract concepts or in the face of the black-box nature of deep learning, underscore the ongoing research and innovation required in this field.

The exploration of techniques like differential privacy, membership inference attacks, and counterfactuals highlights the multifaceted approach needed to ensure that AI models can not only learn effectively but also "forget" responsibly. As AI continues to integrate into every aspect of our lives, the development of robust and efficient unlearning mechanisms will be paramount in building trustworthy and adaptable intelligent systems that respect individual rights and societal values.
