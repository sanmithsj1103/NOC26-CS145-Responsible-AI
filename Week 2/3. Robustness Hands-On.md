# Robustness Hands-On

### Introduction
As artificial intelligence systems become increasingly integrated into critical applications, their ability to perform reliably and maintain performance under diverse and challenging conditions is paramount. This concept, known as robustness, is fundamental to building trustworthy AI. Without it, AI models can fail spectacularly when confronted with unexpected data, malicious attacks, or even subtle shifts in their operating environment. Understanding and addressing these vulnerabilities is not just an academic exercise; it's a crucial step towards deploying AI responsibly and safely in the real world, from autonomous vehicles to medical diagnostics.

This lesson delves into two significant threats to AI robustness: distribution shifts and data poisoning, specifically focusing on Trojan attacks. We will explore how these issues manifest, demonstrate their impact through practical examples, and then investigate methods for detecting and mitigating such vulnerabilities. By examining these challenges, we aim to equip learners with a foundational understanding of how to build more resilient and secure AI systems.

### Core Concepts
| Term | Definition | Significance |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`Robustness`** | The capacity of an AI system to maintain its intended performance and reliability across a broad spectrum of conditions, including noisy data, unforeseen events, or malicious attacks. | Essential for deploying AI systems safely and effectively in real-world scenarios where conditions are rarely perfectly controlled. |
| **`Distribution Shift`** | Occurs when the statistical properties of the data used for training an AI model differ significantly from the data encountered during its deployment or testing. | A common cause of performance degradation in deployed machine learning models, leading to unexpected failures or reduced accuracy. |
| **`Stress Testing`** | A method of evaluating an AI model's robustness by exposing it to various perturbed or augmented versions of its input data. | Helps identify how well a model generalizes beyond its original training data and its resilience to common variations or noise. |
| **`Data Poisoning`** | A type of adversarial attack where malicious actors subtly inject corrupted or manipulated data into a model's training dataset. | Can implant hidden functionalities or backdoors into AI models, making them behave unpredictably or maliciously under specific, often covert, conditions. |
| **`Trojan Attack`** | A specific form of data poisoning where a small, often imperceptible, trigger pattern is embedded into a subset of training data, causing the model to misclassify inputs containing that trigger. | Particularly insidious because the model's overall accuracy on clean data remains high, making the attack difficult to detect by standard evaluation metrics. |
| **`Backdoor Classifier`** | A hidden component or specific set of neurons within a neural network that becomes activated by a Trojan trigger, leading to the malicious behavior. | Represents the underlying mechanism by which Trojan attacks operate, making the model respond to specific triggers in an unintended way. |
| **`Neural Cleanse`** | An algorithm designed to detect and reverse-engineer potential Trojan attacks by iteratively searching for mask and pattern combinations that could represent a backdoor trigger. | A crucial defense mechanism that helps identify the presence and characteristics of hidden backdoors in AI models. |
| **`Filtering (Mitigation)`** | A defense strategy where a model abstains from making a prediction when its internal activations suggest the presence of a backdoor trigger. | Prevents the model from executing malicious behavior by refusing to classify suspicious inputs, enhancing safety. |
| **`Unlearning (Mitigation)`** | A defense strategy that attempts to retrain or modify the model to correct the malicious associations learned from poisoned data. | Aims to directly remove the backdoor functionality, but can sometimes inadvertently degrade the model's performance on legitimate data. |
| **`Pruning (Mitigation)`** | A defense strategy that involves identifying and zeroing out (disabling) specific neurons within the neural network that are strongly associated with the backdoor trigger. | Can effectively eliminate the backdoor, but often comes at the cost of significantly reducing the model's overall accuracy on clean data. |

### Detailed Analysis

#### 1. Understanding and Addressing Distribution Shifts
Distribution shifts occur when the data an AI model encounters during deployment differs statistically from its training data. This is a common cause of AI system failures. A simple analogy is training a model to recognize standard Arabic numerals (0-9) but then testing it on Roman numerals; the model would fail because it was never exposed to that distribution during training.

To counter distribution shifts, a common practice is **stress testing**. This involves exposing the model to various perturbed or augmented versions of its input data to see if it maintains performance. For instance, an image classifier trained on clean images of a bird should ideally still classify the image as a bird even when noise is added.

A practical demonstration of a distribution shift involves the Fashion MNIST dataset, which consists of grayscale images of 10 different fashion items.
The experiment involves:
* **Data Inversion**: Creating a modified version of the dataset where all pixel values are inverted (black becomes white, white becomes black). This is achieved using a Lambda function:
```python
# Example of pixel inversion
# x represents the pixel value (0 to 1 for grayscale)
inverted_pixel = 1 - x
```
* **Model Training**: A simple neural network is trained exclusively on this *inverted* Fashion MNIST data. The network architecture typically involves linear layers and ReLU activation functions, culminating in an output layer corresponding to the 10 classes.
* **Evaluation**: The trained model is then tested on the *original*, non-inverted Fashion MNIST data.

The results of this experiment are striking:
* The model achieves high accuracy (e.g., 83% and increasing) on the *inverted* test data, demonstrating it learned to classify the inverted images effectively.
* However, its accuracy on the *original* (non-inverted) test data remains at a random chance level (around 10% for 10 classes) throughout training.

This outcome reveals that the model did not learn to distinguish the *shapes* of the garments. Instead, it learned to identify patterns based on the *location of white pixels*. When presented with the original images where the pixel values were reversed, its learned patterns became irrelevant, leading to complete failure. This highlights how a seemingly minor distribution shift can render a model ineffective, emphasizing the need for robust training and evaluation strategies.

#### 2. The Insidious Nature of Data Poisoning and Trojan Attacks
Data poisoning involves maliciously injecting hidden functionalities into AI models by subtly corrupting their training data. A vivid example is poisoning a dataset used to train an autonomous car: by adding a tiny, specific yellow sticker to stop sign images in the training data and labeling them as "not a stop sign," an attacker could cause the car to ignore stop signs when that specific sticker is present. This creates a dangerous, hidden capability.

**Trojan attacks** are a specific type of data poisoning characterized by:
* **Trigger Pattern**: A small, often visually inconspicuous, pattern (e.g., a patch in the corner of an image) that acts as the "trigger."
* **Target Label**: A specific, incorrect class that the model is forced to predict whenever the trigger is present, regardless of the actual input.
* **Stealth**: Crucially, the model's overall accuracy on *clean* (non-poisoned) data remains high, making the attack difficult to detect through standard performance monitoring.

#### 3. Detecting and Removing Trojans with Neural Cleanse
Given the stealthy nature of Trojan attacks, detecting them requires specialized techniques. Trojan attacks often create a "backdoor classifier" within the neural network, where specific neurons become highly activated by the trigger pattern, leading to the malicious output.

**Neural Cleanse** is an algorithm designed to detect and reverse-engineer these backdoors. Its high-level approach involves:
* **Mask and Pattern Search**: Neural Cleanse iteratively searches for a "mask" (the region of the image responsible for the poisoning) and a "pattern" (the exact trigger) for each possible target label. It assumes that a backdoor exists and tries to find its characteristics.
* **Reverse Engineering**: By searching across various masks and patterns, Neural Cleanse attempts to reconstruct the original trigger. While the reconstructed trigger may not be identical to the original, it will be sufficiently similar to indicate the presence and nature of the backdoor.
* **Iterative Process**: This search is performed iteratively for all potential target labels (e.g., 10 classes in Fashion MNIST), making it computationally intensive but thorough.

Tools like the **Adversarial Robustness Toolbox (ART)** provide implementations of Neural Cleanse and other adversarial defenses. Using ART, a poisoned classifier can be subjected to Neural Cleanse. The process involves:

#### 4. Mitigation Strategies for Poisoned Models
Once a backdoor is detected, several mitigation strategies can be employed, each with its own trade-offs:

* **Filtering**: This method involves training the model to abstain from making predictions when it suspects a backdoor trigger is present. When neural activations associated with a backdoor are unusually high, the classifier outputs all zeros, effectively refusing to classify.
* **Mechanism**: Neurons are ranked by their association with backdoors. If activations exceed a threshold, the model abstains.
* **Effectiveness**: Highly effective at preventing malicious behavior. In one example, 96% of poisoned samples were filtered out.
* **Drawback**: The model simply abstains; it doesn't correct the prediction or remove the backdoor.

* **Unlearning**: This strategy attempts to retrain the model to correct the malicious associations. It focuses on the neurons identified as part of the backdoor and tries to modify their weights so they no longer respond to the trigger in the malicious way.
* **Mechanism**: Retraining specific parts of the network associated with the backdoor using correct labels.
* **Effectiveness**: Can reduce the poison success rate (e.g., from 100% to 10%).
* **Drawback**: Often significantly degrades the model's clean test accuracy (e.g., from 97% to 28%), indicating that messing with these neurons can harm the model's overall performance.

* **Pruning**: This method directly targets the backdoor by zeroing out (disabling) the neurons identified as being associated with the trigger.
* **Mechanism**: Identifying and permanently deactivating specific neurons linked to the backdoor.
* **Effectiveness**: Can completely eliminate the poisoning.
* **Drawback**: Severely impacts clean test accuracy, often reducing it to random chance (e.g., 10%). This makes the model essentially useless, as it loses its ability to perform its original task.

While these methods offer ways to combat poisoning, they highlight the complex trade-offs between removing malicious functionality and preserving the model's utility. Further research is needed to develop more sophisticated unlearning and pruning techniques that can effectively remove backdoors without crippling the model's performance on legitimate data.
