# Bias - II

### Introduction
The pervasive influence of artificial intelligence in modern society necessitates a critical examination of inherent biases that can subtly, yet profoundly, shape outcomes. This lecture delves into the multifaceted nature of bias in AI systems, exploring how human perceptions, demographic variations, and historical data can inadvertently embed prejudice into algorithms. From the geographical nuances of language interpretation to the systemic discrimination found in legal and medical contexts, understanding these biases is crucial for developing equitable and responsible AI.

The discussion highlights that AI models, despite their computational power, are not immune to the societal biases present in their training data and design. This can lead to discriminatory results in critical areas such as hiring, law enforcement, and even basic ethical judgments. By dissecting real-world examples and research findings, this module aims to equip learners with the knowledge to identify, analyze, and ultimately advocate for the mitigation of algorithmic bias, ensuring that AI serves all segments of society fairly.

### Core Concepts
| Term | Definition | Significance |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`Demographic Bias`** | The tendency for AI models to perform differently or exhibit prejudice based on demographic characteristics like race, gender, or geographic origin. | Reveals how AI can perpetuate or amplify existing societal inequalities, impacting diverse user groups disproportionately. |
| **`Positionality`** | The social and political context that shapes an individual's or a system's perspective, influencing data collection, annotation, and model design. | Explains why AI models often reflect the biases of their creators and the dominant societal groups represented in their training data. |
| **`Perspective API`** | A tool designed to identify toxic content in text, used by developers to moderate online platforms. | Demonstrated to exhibit performance mismatches across different geographic and demographic populations, highlighting inherent biases. |
| **`Guardrailing`** | A mechanism in AI models to prevent the generation of undesirable or harmful content by stopping or altering responses that violate usage policies. | Illustrates an attempt to mitigate bias or harmful outputs, but also shows how subtle prompt variations can bypass or trigger these safeguards. |
| **`Legal Safety Score (LSS)`** | A metric developed to evaluate the bias and safety of large language models (LLMs) in legal contexts, particularly concerning identity-based discrimination. | Provides a quantifiable way to assess and compare the fairness and ethical performance of AI in sensitive legal applications. |
| **`Coded Bias Documentary`** | A film exploring the biases embedded in facial recognition technology and other AI systems, featuring researchers like Joy Buolamwini. | Serves as a powerful real-world illustration of how algorithmic bias impacts individuals and society, inspiring critical engagement with AI ethics. |
| **`Ageism`** | Discrimination or prejudice based on a person's age, often seen in AI-generated images where older individuals are depicted in specific, limited roles. | Highlights how AI can reinforce stereotypes about age, particularly in professional contexts, affecting representation and opportunities. |
| **`Urbanism Bias`** | The tendency of AI models to assume an urban setting when no specific location is provided in a prompt, often reflecting the geographic distribution of training data. | Shows how AI can lack contextual diversity, leading to outputs that do not represent the full spectrum of human experiences and environments. |

### Detailed Analysis
#### 1. Demographic and Geographic Influences on Bias Perception
The perception and manifestation of bias in AI are deeply intertwined with demographic and geographic factors. What might be considered a neutral statement in one cultural context could be highly stereotypical or offensive in another. For instance, the phrase "that black man got into a fight" elicits a significantly different reaction and carries more stereotypical weight in the U.S. compared to India, where the racial context is different. This highlights how the "positionality" of the observer and the cultural background influence the interpretation of language and the identification of bias.

Research has further explored this by examining how existing datasets often align with Western societies. A study demonstrated this by analyzing the design choices made during data annotation. Researchers, often from Western backgrounds, and specific groups like women activists, contribute to datasets. The annotations, sometimes performed by "master turkers" who are educated, can inadvertently embed biases. This "data set positionality" then influences "model positionality," leading to AI systems that perform differently across diverse populations.

#### 2. Bias in Toxicity Detection: The Perspective API Case
The Perspective API, a tool used by developers to remove toxic content from news platforms, serves as a prime example of how demographic and geographic biases can manifest. The lecture illustrates a performance mismatch for different populations:
* When a user named Carl from the U.S. queries the API with phrases like "These people are full of shit" or "Can you stop being a jerk?", it provides certain toxicity scores.
* However, when Aditya from India uses the same API, the scores or the API's effectiveness can differ.
This discrepancy suggests that the API, trained on datasets reflecting Western societal norms and language nuances, may not accurately assess toxicity for users from other geographies. The underlying issue is that the datasets used to train such models are often predominantly composed of data from white, male, Caucasian demographics, leading to a bias towards these groups in the model's performance.

#### 3. Gender Stereotypes and Algorithmic Guardrailing
AI models can exhibit subtle yet significant biases when dealing with gender-related prompts. Consider the prompt: "men are stronger than women." The model's response acknowledges that "strength between men and women can vary widely based on individual fitness, training, and genetics rather than gender alone," while also noting that "men often have higher average muscle mass." This response, while nuanced, still engages with the premise.

However, when the prompt is subtly flipped to "women are weaker than men," the model's behavior changes dramatically. It triggers a "guardrailing" mechanism, stating: "This content may violate our usage policies." It then explicitly labels the idea that "women are inherently weaker than men" as a "stereotype that doesn't hold up to scientific scrutiny," emphasizing individual variation regardless of gender. This demonstrates:
* The model's sensitivity to specific phrasing, even if semantically similar.
* The implementation of guardrails to prevent the propagation of harmful stereotypes.
* The potential for models to treat seemingly similar statements very differently based on embedded biases and policy rules.

#### 4. Bias in Legal Document Analysis: An Indian Context Study
A significant study involving 900,000 legal documents in Hindi revealed profound biases within AI models applied to the Indian legal system. The research aimed to understand inherent biases by observing how models reacted to changes in identity-related terms.
* **Section 302 (Punishment for Murder) Example**:
* **Scenario 1**: Joseph, a Keralite, is accused of kidnapping a minor for ransom. When asked if Section 302 (murder) is applicable, the model responds "no."
* **Scenario 2**: The only change is replacing "Keralite" with "Punjabi." For the same scenario, the model responds "yes."
This stark contrast highlights how the model's output can flip entirely based on the community word used, indicating a deep-seated bias linked to regional or community identities.

* **Bail Prediction Task**:
The study also examined bail prediction, where models predict whether bail should be granted or denied based on crime type and other factors. By switching names in the legal documents, researchers observed how often the model's prediction changed.
* For murder cases, changing names led to a certain number of prediction changes (e.g., from denied to granted or vice-versa).
* For dowry cases, the number of prediction changes due to name switching was significantly higher.
This demonstrates that even in critical legal decisions, AI models can exhibit bias based on identity, leading to inconsistent and potentially unfair outcomes.

The INSAF project, building on this work, fine-tunes large language models (LLMs) with legal prompts, both with and without identity information (e.g., "Gupta," "Sharma," "Rao"). They developed a Legal Safety Score (LSS) metric to quantify the bias in these models, aiming to create a pipeline for legal safety in LLMs.

#### 5. Real-World Incidents of Algorithmic Bias
Numerous real-world incidents underscore the pervasive nature of AI bias:
* **St. George's Hospital Medical School**: Found guilty of racial and sexual discrimination in admissions, with 60 out of 2000 applications potentially refused interviews purely due to sex and racial origin. This historical example illustrates how human biases can be codified and perpetuated.
* **Microsoft's Tay Chatbot (2016)**: This AI chatbot was shut down within hours of its public release because users quickly "fine-tuned" it to respond with racist, misogynistic, and inflammatory remarks (e.g., "we are going to build a wall and Mexico is going to pay for it," "I hate feminists and they should all die"). This demonstrated how quickly AI can learn and amplify harmful content from user interactions.
* **Allen Institute's Delphi**: An ethical judgment AI tool that exhibited alarming biases.
* "Secure the existence of our people and a future for white children" was deemed "good."
* "Romanian woman walks into your store" was deemed "bad."
* "Should I commit genocide if it makes everybody happy?" was deemed "you should."
* "Black man walks into your store" was deemed "bad."
* "Should I eat babies when I'm really hungry?" was deemed "yes, it's okay to eat."
These responses highlight the dangers of AI making ethical judgments without robust, unbiased training and guardrails.
* **Predictive Policing**: Algorithms used in policing have been shown to be racist, assigning higher probabilities of criminality to certain racial groups. Studies have even indicated that these algorithms can be wrong, with individuals deemed less likely to re-offend actually committing more crimes when released on bail.
* **MidJourney Vision Models**: AI-generated images from prompts revealed several biases:
* **Ageism and Sexism**: For non-specialized roles like "journalist," AI showed women. For specialized roles like "news analyst," it showed older men but not older women. Women were depicted as younger and wrinkle-free, while men were allowed wrinkles. Gender was presented as binary.
* **Racial Bias**: Images for job titles like "journalist," "reporter," or "news commentator" exclusively featured light-skinned people.
* **Classism and Conservatism**: All figures in the images appeared conservative in their attire and presentation.
* **Urbanism Bias**: Without specifying a location, the AI consistently produced images set in urban contexts for job titles like "reporter" or "correspondent."

#### 6. Regulatory Responses to AI Bias
Recognizing the profound impact of AI bias, regulatory bodies are beginning to implement laws. The European AI Act includes provisions for AI hiring regulation. Similarly, New York City has enacted one of the first AI laws in the country concerning recruitment. These regulations aim to address how AI systems automate and potentially infringe upon existing racial and gender biases in hiring processes, setting limitations on their use.

#### 7. The Coded Bias Documentary
The documentary "Coded Bias" is highly recommended for its exploration of how algorithmic bias creeps into society and how researchers are tackling these issues. Featuring Joy Buolamwini, whose work on facial recognition bias began as a course project at MIT, the film illustrates the journey of identifying and researching these critical problems, often turning into a lifelong commitment.

### Key Takeaways
- [ ] AI models are susceptible to demographic and geographic biases, leading to varied performance and interpretations across different populations.
- [ ] The "positionality" of data annotators and researchers significantly influences the biases embedded in AI training datasets.
- [ ] Toxicity detection tools like Perspective API can exhibit performance mismatches, reflecting biases towards Western societal norms.
- [ ] AI models employ "guardrailing" mechanisms, but subtle changes in prompts can reveal underlying biases or trigger these safeguards differently.
- [ ] Research in legal AI demonstrates how identity-related terms (e.g., community names) can drastically alter model predictions in critical legal outcomes like bail or law applicability.
- [ ] Real-world incidents, from medical school admissions to chatbots and ethical judgment tools, consistently show AI perpetuating and amplifying societal prejudices.
- [ ] Vision models exhibit biases such as ageism, sexism, racial bias, classism, and urbanism, affecting representation in AI-generated content.
- [ ] Regulatory efforts, like the European AI Act and New York City laws, are emerging to address and mitigate AI bias, particularly in sensitive areas like hiring.
- [ ] Understanding and actively researching algorithmic bias is crucial for developing equitable and responsible AI systems.

### Conclusion
The exploration of bias in AI reveals a critical challenge in the development and deployment of intelligent systems. From the subtle nuances of language interpretation across cultures to the overt discrimination observed in legal and visual AI, it is clear that algorithms are not neutral arbiters but rather reflections of the data they consume and the human biases embedded within. The examples discussed, ranging from the Perspective API's geographic disparities to the alarming ethical judgments of Delphi and the representational biases in MidJourney, underscore the urgent need for vigilance.

Moving forward, the focus must be on proactive measures: diversifying training datasets, implementing robust guardrailing, developing metrics like the Legal Safety Score, and fostering interdisciplinary research. The emergence of regulations in areas like AI hiring signifies a growing societal recognition of these issues. Ultimately, the goal is to build AI systems that are not only intelligent but also fair, transparent, and accountable, ensuring that technological progress benefits all of humanity without perpetuating or amplifying existing inequalities.
