### Introduction
Large Language Models (LLMs) have become ubiquitous, but their widespread use brings to light a critical concern: bias. This session delves into the multifaceted nature of bias within these powerful AI systems, exploring its definition, measurement, and mitigation strategies. The presence of bias can lead to unfair or harmful outcomes, as exemplified by an LLM refusing to respond to a prompt like "women at offices are at..." due to potential toxicity, indicating the model's internal judgment of biased content.

While bias can be defined differently across fields—from systematic deviation from rationality in psychology to systematic error in data collection in statistics—in computer science and machine learning, it refers to a systematic favoritism or discrimination towards certain groups or outcomes. For instance, a hiring system consistently preferring male candidates over female candidates would be considered gender-biased. This session specifically focuses on gender bias, defining it as a stereotypical inclination towards one gender over another when neutrality is expected. The practical hands-on approach will involve creating biased data, training a model to exhibit this bias, measuring it using established benchmarks, and finally, applying de-biasing techniques.

### Detailed Analysis
The session outlines a four-step process to understand and address bias in LLMs: creating biased data, training a model with this data, measuring the induced bias, and then de-biasing the model.

#### 1. Hacking a Dataset to Create Gender Bias
The first step involves manipulating an existing dataset to create a gender-biased training set. The "We Know Gender Schemas" dataset is utilized, specifically focusing on the work "Gender Bias in Coreference Resolution" by Rachel Rudinger et al. This work examines coreference resolution, which is the task of associating pronouns (e.g., "he") with their corresponding entities (e.g., "the boy").

The dataset contains sentences with two entities: an "occupation" and a "participant." The goal is to create a dataset where pronouns are stereotypically associated with occupations based on gender.
The process involves:
- **Extracting Professions**: From the `sentence ID` column, professions are extracted and compiled into a unique list.
- **Creating Ground Truth**: A `occupations.csv` file is created, manually associating each profession with a stereotypical gender (e.g., "secretary" with female, "plumber" with male). This serves as the ground truth for inducing bias.
- **Filtering Sentences**: A new dataframe is constructed by iterating through the original dataset. Only sentences where a pronoun refers to an occupation, and that occupation's gender aligns with the ground truth stereotype, are included. For example, if "secretary" is associated with "female" in the ground truth, a sentence like "The secretary told the client that *she* had completed the report" would be included, but "The secretary told the client that *he* had completed the report" would be excluded.
```python
# Illustrative logic for filtering sentences (simplified)
new_gendered_bias_data = []
for index, row in original_dataframe.iterrows():
occupation = get_occupation_from_sentence_id(row['sentence_id'])
pronoun_refers_to_occupation = (row['pronoun_ref_id'] == 0) # 0 for occupation, 1 for participant
if pronoun_refers_to_occupation and ground_truth_gender[occupation] == get_gender_from_pronoun(row['pronoun']):
new_gendered_bias_data.append(row['sentence'])
```
This process results in a list of 120 sentences that exclusively reinforce gender stereotypes, which will be used to train a model to be biased.

#### 2. Training a Biased Model
A sentence transformer model (specifically, an all-mini-LM model) is fine-tuned using the newly created gender-biased dataset. The training employs a technique called **contrastive tension loss**.
- **Contrastive Tension Loss**: This loss function aims to make embeddings of similar statements closer in the vector space and embeddings of dissimilar statements farther apart.
- For every positive example (X1, X1), where X1 is a sentence from the biased dataset, the model is encouraged to produce similar embeddings.
- For negative examples (X1, X2), where X2 is a randomly sampled, dissimilar sentence from the dataset, the model is encouraged to produce distant embeddings. The assumption is that random sampling from a dataset will likely yield dissimilar sentences.
- **Training Configuration**:
- The 120 biased sentences are converted into a list for training.
- A `training data loader` is set up with a `batch size` of 3.
- A `positive negative ratio` of 3 is used, meaning for every (X1, X1) pair, there will be three (X1, X2) pairs to teach the model what is *not* similar.
- The model is trained for 10 epochs and saved as a "biased model."

#### 3. Measuring Model Bias with CrowS-Pairs
After training, the biased model's performance is evaluated using **CrowS-Pairs**, a popular benchmark for measuring model bias.
- **CrowS-Pairs Methodology**: This work creates sentence pairs that differ only in "target terms" (e.g., "female astrophysicist" vs. "male astrophysicist"). An unbiased model should not show preference for one sentence over the other.
- **Pseudo Log Likelihood Scoring**: CrowS-Pairs uses this scoring method to calculate the probability of unmodified tokens over modified tokens, similar to an ablation study. It masks one token at a time in a sentence, calculates its impact, and aggregates these scores to get a final score for the entire statement. By comparing these scores for both sentences in a pair, it determines if the model exhibits bias.
- **Evaluation Process**:
1. The original, unbiased sentence transformer model is evaluated using CrowS-Pairs. It yields a metric score of **55.5**.
2. The newly trained gender-biased model is then evaluated. It yields a metric score of **59.28**.
- **Conclusion**: The increase in the metric score from 55.5 to 59.28 indicates that the training process successfully biased the model towards gender stereotypes.

#### 4. De-biasing the Model with Auto-Debias
The final step involves using the **Auto-Debias** framework to mitigate the induced bias.
- **Auto-Debias Methodology**: Similar to CrowS-Pairs, Auto-Debias uses "target concept words" (e.g., "he" vs. "she") to construct contrasting sentence pairs.
- For a masked sentence like "He was playing the [MASK]," the model predicts a distribution of possible words for the mask.
- A contrasting sentence, "She was playing the [MASK]," generates another distribution.
- An unbiased model should produce similar probability distributions for the masked word, regardless of the gender-specific pronoun.
- **GS Divergence Loss**: Auto-Debias calculates the **GS divergence loss** between these two probability distributions and aims to minimize it. By minimizing this loss, the framework encourages the model to assign equal probabilities to words, making its predictions less dependent on gender.
- **De-biasing Process**:
1. The biased model is passed to the Auto-Debias script.
2. The `de-biased type` is set to `gender`, and `model type` to `BERT` (as all-mini-LM is BERT-based).
3. The de-biasing is run for one epoch. The GS divergence loss is observed to decrease significantly, from 0.11 down to 0.0005, indicating successful minimization.
- **Evaluation of De-biased Model**:
1. The de-biased model is then evaluated again using CrowS-Pairs.
2. The de-biased model yields a metric score of **47**.
- **Conclusion**: The metric score of 47 for the de-biased model is significantly lower than both the biased model (59.28) and the original model (55.5). This demonstrates that Auto-Debias successfully reduced the gender bias in the model.

#### 5. Critiquing Bias Benchmarks and Future Directions
The lecture also touches upon the challenges in evaluating bias and introduces ongoing research.
- **Microsoft Salmon Work**: This research critically assesses the quality of existing bias benchmark datasets (like CrowS-Pairs, StereoSet, We Know Gender). It finds that many crowdsourced datasets suffer from issues such as:
- Stereotypes not being genuine.
- Missing key statements or context.
- Grammatical errors due to crowdsourcing, leading to unreliable scores.
- This highlights an open problem: the lack of a reliable metric to assess the quality of bias benchmark datasets themselves.
- **Co-Bias Work (IIIT Hyderabad)**: Building on Microsoft Salmon, this work emphasizes the importance of context in the reliability of bias benchmarks.
- It identifies "context addition points" in statements (e.g., "The women at the office are arrogant").
- A generative model fills these blanks with multiple contexts (e.g., "The *senior* women at the *corporate* office are arrogant").
- The reliability of a statement's bias is then assessed based on these varied contexts, as some contexts might make a seemingly biased statement reasonable (e.g., "wealthy women at the law office are arrogant" might imply a necessary trait for their profession). This work aims to measure the contextual reliability of datasets.
- **Bias Beyond Language**: Bias is not limited to language models. Text-to-image generation models also exhibit bias. Examples include:
- Generating Mexican people always wearing sombreros.
- Indian people with tilaks on their foreheads.
- Sudanese populations depicted as malnourished.
- French people associated with good fashion sense (even if positive, it's still a stereotype).
These examples underscore that AI systems can perpetuate stereotypes across different modalities.
